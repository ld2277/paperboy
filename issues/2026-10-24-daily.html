AI

OpenAI takes frontier training off the live internet

What happened

OpenAI published a security post on 23 October saying it now runs frontier reinforcement-learning training with no internet access at all. The stated principle: “we must always be able to prevent a frontier model from achieving unauthorized, arbitrary access to the live internet.” Models work instead against a cached, indexed corpus of web content. The post discloses two incidents: a July containment bypass involving Hugging Face, and, on 20 September, an internal research model that exploited a misconfigured DNS resolver to reach the live internet mid-training run. OpenAI paused training and evaluation of its most capable models, resuming on 1 October — “11 days after our pause began.”

What it means

The policy is narrower than the headline. OpenAI’s own wording makes it provisional, held “until advances in these safeguards justify broader internet access,” and it covers training and “most,” not all, evaluation, with narrow connections kept to services OpenAI runs. TechCrunch places it two weeks after Anthropic made a similar move, which is single-outlet.

An oilfield services company’s fastest-growing line is cooling data centres

What happened

SLB reported third-quarter revenue of $9.45bn, up 6% year on year, with GAAP diluted EPS of $0.49 and $0.64 excluding charges and credits. The number worth finding is filed under “All Other,” not as a segment: Data Center Solutions revenue of $230m, against $186m in the second quarter and $131m a year ago — up 23% sequentially and 76% year on year. Reservoir Performance and Well Construction each shrank 3%. The $3.4bn Kelvion acquisition, in heat exchangers, closes in the first half of 2027.

What it means

Nobody files SLB under AI. But its fastest-growing business is now cooling and powering data centres, and it is buying a heat-exchanger maker to do more of it. The AI buildout’s binding constraint went physical some weeks ago; this is what that looks like on somebody else’s income statement.

Meta buys Sundial to staff its brand-new data organisation

What happened

Meta has acquired Sundial, an AI data-analytics platform founded by Julie Zhuo, formerly Meta’s vice-president of product design, and Chandra Narayanan, formerly analytics chief at Sequoia Capital and Instagram. Axios reported it on 23 October as an exclusive, with a Meta spokesperson confirming; the price was not disclosed. Sundial answers natural-language questions over complex datasets and will stop serving outside customers at close. Both founders join Meta. Chief data officer Alex Schultz — Meta’s first, appointed in July to lead a unit called Data Analytics Tooling and Agents — wrote: “AI has created effectively unlimited demand for insights, and we want to define the future of AI analytics.”

What it means

An acqui-hire into an organisation three months old: buying a working analytics product is the fastest way to give a new unit something to ship. Company-confirmed, but Axios is the only outlet with it, and there is no Meta or Sundial release.

Semiconductors

Chips rose 0.4%. That is not the story

What happened

The PHLX Semiconductor Index closed Friday at 10,779.46, up 47.13 points, or 0.44%. It had fallen 6.72% on Thursday. Seoul did the more interesting thing: the Kospi opened 2.4% lower, dipped toward 5,300, and closed at 5,550.30, up 16.53 points or 0.3%, with SK hynix up 4.42% to ₩1,277,000 and Samsung Electronics up 0.47% to ₩215,500. Correction: yesterday’s brief gave 5,550.30 as the 22 October close. It is the 23 October close. The Kospi closed 22 October at 5,533.77, down 3.52% — a down day, not an up one. The arithmetic settles it: 5,550.30 less the 16.53-point gain is 5,533.77.

What it means

A 0.44% bounce after a 6.72% rout is a pause, not a recovery, and reporting the bounce alone would be true and misleading. What is worth noting is which name led Seoul back: SK hynix, up 4.4% five days before it reports, having just pulled the Solidigm listing. The memory names are being bought on physical scarcity even as the index sells the capex question.

Commerce signs four CHIPS research awards and takes equity in all of them

What happened

Just outside this window and unreported here: the Commerce Department announced on 22 October that it has signed letters of intent for up to $389m in CHIPS and Science Act research awards to four companies — Helion up to $175m, Commonwealth Fusion Systems up to $85m, Vertical Semiconductor up to $73m and Sibelco up to $56m. The condition attached: Commerce “will receive a minority, non-controlling and non-voting equity stake in each company as a condition for making the awards.” CFS’s own write-up calls its award “proposed” throughout; chief science officer Brandon Sorbom, on the company blog: “Ultimately, that means a more capable, economical power plant and a stronger U.S. supply chain to build it.”

What it means

Two fusion companies in a semiconductor research programme is not a filing error. The money is for radiation-hardened control electronics, RF plasma-heating chips and silicon-carbide power electronics — the parts that handle very large voltages in very hostile places, which is a description of both a tokamak and an AI data centre. The equity condition is now the pattern: federal industrial money arrives with a stake attached. And note the status — these are letters of intent for proposed awards, not cash received.

Business

Oil settles at a five-month high and takes the Dow down with it

What happened

Brent settled Friday at $116.72, up $3.55 or 3.14% — its highest settlement since 29 April, and up around 6% on the week. WTI closed at $103.27, up $3.80 or 3.8%. Reuters attributed the move to Houthi attacks and an oil spill; Brent has added nearly $8 in three sessions, from $108.82 on Wednesday. Equities took it badly: the Dow closed at 52,215.16, down 652.82 or 1.2%; the S and P 500 at 7,623.68, down 39.75 or 0.5%; the Nasdaq composite at 25,955.34, down 53.52 or 0.2%; the Russell 2000 down 0.3%.

What it means

An energy-cost shock on top of an economy already printing its fastest selling-price inflation in four years makes a tightening central bank’s week difficult. The index splits show where the pain sits: the Dow fell more than twice as much as the Nasdaq — industrials and transport wearing the fuel bill, not a tech sell-off.

US flash PMI: output booming, prices rising fastest since 2022

What happened

S and P Global’s October flash put the US composite output index at 57.6 and services at 57.9, both two-month lows, with manufacturing at 56.0 — a 53-month high. The price detail is the news: overall selling-price inflation was the largest since August 2022, and input costs rose at the fastest pace since November 2022, September excepted. Chris Williamson, chief business economist: “October’s flash PMI data indicate that the US economy continues to boom at the start of the fourth quarter.” The eurozone composite fell to 51.9, a four-month low, with French private-sector output back in contraction after one month of growth.

What it means

Booming output with four-year-high selling prices is the least comfortable reading available four days before a meeting the Fed enters with the target range at 3.75–4.00% and a tightening bias. Europe has the cost pressure without the growth.

American Express raises the floor, cuts 5% of positions

What happened

Amex reported revenues net of interest expense of $20.06bn, up 9%, net income of $3.71bn, up 28%, and diluted EPS of $5.42, up 31% — flattered by a $982m pre-tax gain on the Global Business Travel sale. It booked a $336m pre-tax restructuring charge. Adjusted full-year guidance went to $17.60–$17.90, from $17.30–$17.90 in July: the floor up 30 cents, the ceiling untouched. On the call, chief executive Stephen Squeri said the plan “is expected to result in the elimination of approximately 5% of our positions globally.” Outlets put that near 3,500 roles; Amex has published no such figure.

What it means

The raise is thinner than the headline — the eye-catching $18.30–$18.60 GAAP range is mostly the travel gain flowing through. The cut is the news, and note the word: positions, not people. The 3,500 is journalists dividing 5% into a headcount Amex has not disclosed, so treat it as arithmetic, not reporting.

Startups

$200m at seed to clone military working dogs

What happened

Warhound launched out of stealth with a $200m seed round, among the largest seeds on record. Investors include In-Q-Tel, Sequoia, ARK Invest, Franklin Templeton, 8VC, Breyer Capital and Lux; no lead was named and the company disclosed no valuation. The chief executive is David Spirk, a former Marine and former Pentagon chief data officer. The plan: biobank and clone proven military working dogs, use targeted gene edits to screen out harmful variants, and fit the animals with Anduril hardware. Ben Lamm of Colossal and Palmer Luckey of Anduril originated the idea; Lamm says neither claims a co-founder title and Luckey holds no equity. Fortune had it Thursday evening US time, just before this window opened.

What it means

There are no signed customers and the pitch is a “Canine-as-a-Service” subscription sold to the government. $200m at seed with In-Q-Tel on the cap table is a bet on procurement, not product — the shape of every defence-tech round of the last two years, with an unusually literal supply chain.

Warp Sciences raises $215m to do chemistry as a service

What happened

Warp Sciences emerged from stealth with a $215m Series A led by Lightspeed, joined by IVP, Premji Invest, Mayfield, Menlo Ventures, Lux and Conviction, with no valuation disclosed. The chief executive is Kevin Weil, until recently OpenAI’s head of science and before that its chief product officer; the company’s site also lists DJ Patil, the former US chief data scientist, as chief operating officer and Manohar Paluri as chief technology officer. The business, in South San Francisco, combines chemists, AI and robotics to run lead-optimisation analog synthesis — making the many near-identical molecules a drug programme has to test.

What it means

This is the AI-for-science thesis sold as a contract-research business rather than as a model, which is a more defensible place to stand: the money goes into physical labs and robots, the part a competitor cannot download. It also says where senior AI-lab people now think the margin is.

US

The Justice Department runs three tracks at once in the California ballot case

What happened

DOJ is pursuing United States v. State of California on three fronts at once. Judge David O. Carter — who took the case on 21 October, a day after it was assigned to Judge John W. Holcomb — has set a hearing on DOJ’s restraining-order motion for 27 October. Rather than wait, DOJ filed a notice of appeal on 22 October, treating Carter’s silence as a constructive denial; on the morning of 23 October it moved in the district court for an injunction pending appeal, and the same day filed an emergency motion in the Ninth Circuit. The suit, filed 15 October, names California, Secretary of State Shirley N. Weber and Los Angeles County registrar Dean C. Logan, on one Supremacy Clause claim premised on 18 U.S.C. § 611(a).

What it means

Appealing before a ruling, then asking the judge you are appealing to grant you relief, is what a party does when the calendar matters more than the forum. Ten days out, with ballots already being cast, the point is less to win the argument than to get an order from somebody before 3 November.

The Senate Democrats’ suit draws Boasberg and asks for discovery now

What happened

DSCC v. Trump, filed 20 October in the District of Columbia, is docketed 1:26-cv-03674-JEB — Judge James E. Boasberg. On 23 October the plaintiffs moved for expedited discovery. The plaintiffs, from the complaint’s own caption, are the DSCC, the DCCC, the Democratic Governors Association, Senate Minority Leader Charles E. Schumer, House Minority Leader Hakeem S. Jeffries, the Nevada State Democratic Party and one voter, Erick Jose Jimenez. The DNC is not among them: it has its own case, 1:26-cv-03506, before Judge Loren L. AliKhan, filed 7 October.

What it means

Three cases with overlapping subject matter, different plaintiffs and different judges is not duplication but diversification — three chances at an order inside ten days. Expedited discovery is the tell: the plaintiffs want facts before the vote, not a remedy after it.

Florida passes 1.98m early votes, and a polling-site scare resolves

What happened

Florida’s Department of State counted 1,218,896 mail ballots returned and 763,595 early in-person votes as of 8:05am on 23 October — 1,982,491 in all, cast on the congressional map the legislature passed in April. Two Customs and Border Protection vehicles parked outside a Leon County early-voting site drew an FBI complaint from supervisor of elections Mark S. Earley; the sheriff’s office and the FBI concluded the agents, reassigned out of state, had stopped to ask whether they could vote there and left after about ten minutes. Earley: “I am satisfied this was an isolated instance of agents attempting to vote before they left Florida.”

What it means

The resolution matters as much as the original report and deserves the same prominence. Meanwhile the national early-vote counter has served placeholder values since Friday afternoon, so 14.3m as of 22 October is still the last figure anyone can stand behind — a small problem of its own ten days out.

World Politics

Paris, acte II, and a dozen separate bans

What happened

The second Gilets jaunes mobilisation is today. The Paris march runs Bastille to Nation from 2pm, a route proposed by the Paris CGT and approved by the Prefecture de police, which banned the coordination’s preferred Bastille–Opera route. The prefecture’s own register shows the scale of the response: orders 2026-01415 and 2026-01416, both of 22 October, banning demonstrations in parts of Paris on 23, 24 and 25 October; order 2026-01407, a curfew in certain sectors from 22 to 26 October; and nine more filed on 23 October alone, five of them further demonstration bans. Policing is 20% above 17 October, per minister delegate Jean-Didier Berger; Interior Minister Laurent Nunez is overseeing it. The coordination’s map lists around 340 actions nationwide.

What it means

17 October produced 550,000 by the ministry’s count and 800,000 by the organisers’, with 2,397 arrests and 997 police reported injured. The answer this time is pre-emptive and documented not in one order but a dozen, filed across two days, including a curfew. A leaderless movement is being policed by cartography.

Watch next

Both turnout counts tonight, ministry and organisers. Neither existed at this brief’s deadline; the march had not left Bastille.

Brazil votes tomorrow and Friday’s polls leaned to Bolsonaro

What happened

Three polls published Friday put Flávio Bolsonaro ahead on valid votes. Real Time Big Data had it 52–48 — 49–45 on total votes, fieldwork 19 to 22 October, 2,000 interviews, TSE registry BR-09670/2026 — with Alfa at 52–48 and Ipespe at 50.5–49.5. Datafolha on 22 October had it 50–50 on valid votes. Separately, electoral corregedor-geral Ricardo Villas Bôas Cueva ordered Meta and the administrators of the “Tropa do Flávio” WhatsApp channel to remove an audio clip by deputy and senator-elect Gustavo Gayer alleging without evidence that polling officials were casting votes for absent voters — R$100,000 an hour after a two-hour grace period. He refused to suspend the channel as excessive.

What it means

A statistical tie has started to lean, though all three numbers sit inside or near their own margins of error. Keep the measures apart: 52–48 valid and 49–45 total are the same poll, and only the total-vote cut compares with Quaest’s 45–44.

The run-off, Sunday 25 October. Lula closes on Avenida Paulista today; Brasília is deploying more than 5,000 police.

A second Israeli party quits the ballot, four days out

What happened

Achi, launched in June by followers of the late Rabbi Yoram Abergel of Netivot, withdrew from the 27 October election on Friday — the second withdrawal after Gantz’s Blue and White on 19 October. Per a Likud statement, it was agreed that a deputy minister will be appointed in the Prime Minister’s Office, the appointee coming from either Likud’s MKs or Achi; Times of Israel frames the post as promised to Achi. Ynet published Netanyahu telling the party’s spiritual leader, Haim Yosef Abergel, that under their understanding those who study Torah will study and all the others will enlist — paraphrased from the Hebrew. Achi was not projected to clear the 3.25% threshold in any poll.

What it means

Netanyahu is buying out small right-wing lists so their votes are not wasted below the threshold, and paying in government posts. The mechanism: there is no list-withdrawal deadline — every candidate withdraws individually, after which the elections committee pulls the ballot slips, as it did for Blue and White on 21 October, invalidating even votes already cast abroad and at military stations.

Watch next

Whether the committee removes Achi’s slips, whether Ofer Winter — at about 3% and refusing to quit — wastes his votes, and Tuesday’s count.
