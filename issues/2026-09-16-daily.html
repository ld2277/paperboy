AI

OpenAI puts the three-lab safety talks on the record. The other two labs say nothing.

What happened

OpenAI's global policy chief Chris Lehane told reporters Tuesday that the company has spent weeks coordinating with Anthropic and Google DeepMind on AI safety, the first on-record confirmation of talks this brief has been tracking since July. Lehane's reasoning, verbatim: "It's better to try to work together to prioritize safety" and "There are many instances over time where companies are trying to help each other on safety" (Bloomberg wire, 15 Sept). He said OpenAI backs the provision in the FRONTIER Act that would let "independent verification organizations" operate inside frontier labs, and Sam Altman said OpenAI would embed third-party evaluators (TechCrunch). Anthropic and Google DeepMind did not respond to either outlet. The industry standards body reported alongside this is The Information's sourcing, not Lehane's.

What it means

This is one company narrating a three-company arrangement, which is not the same as three companies agreeing to one. Lehane's antitrust position — that no government waiver is needed — also quietly contradicts Dario Amodei's pacing essay, which asked for a narrow exemption precisely because coordination on capability restraint looks like collusion. The gap between those two readings is the whole regulatory question.

Whether Anthropic or DeepMind confirms on the record; a bill number and markup date for the FRONTIER Act; METR's eight-week report.

Beijing calls the slowdown campaign self-serving, and says a framework without China is not global

What happened

Chinese state media answered the Western pacing campaign directly. A China Daily editorial dated 14 September argued that "A global AI-safety framework that excludes China is not quite global," naming Amodei, Altman and Elon Musk, and cast US policy as moving from hardware curbs — chips, lithography, equipment — to software controls as a containment strategy. Foreign Ministry spokesman Guo Jiakun added: "Fearmongering, confrontation and vicious competition will only disrupt the process of global AI governance" (Bloomberg wire, via republisher). Read through a republisher; Bloomberg is not fetchable here.

What it means

The editorial is doing something more precise than nationalist noise. It identifies the actual weakness in a voluntary, US-led pacing regime: it binds only the labs that sign it, and the unsigned frontier is roughly six months behind and closing. Every argument for restraint made in Washington this month has been met with the same one-line rebuttal, and nobody has answered it.

Semiconductors

SK hynix is reportedly in talks to make memory in America, inside Intel's half-built Ohio plant

What happened

SK hynix is exploring making memory chips in the United States for the first time, in talks with Intel over two structures: leasing part of Intel's long-delayed Ohio complex, or a joint venture with Intel and large cloud buyers to lock in supply. SK hynix currently produces no memory wafers in the US, though it is building a $4bn advanced-packaging plant in Indiana. The company said it is "reviewing various options, including additional production bases" and that nothing is decided; Intel declined to comment on the talks (Korea Herald, 16 Sept). Reuters-originated and single-outlet — unconfirmed. Intel closed Tuesday at $97.14 and was quoted up 4.75% pre-market Wednesday on the report.

What it means

Two problems solving each other. Intel has a site it cannot fill and a foundry business short of anchor tenants; SK hynix has HBM demand it cannot serve from Korea fast enough and a tariff exposure it would rather not carry. If a hyperscaler underwrites it, this is the first memory fab commitment on US soil at scale — and the first sign Ohio becomes an asset rather than a write-down.

Any confirmation from either company; Micron's fiscal Q1 on 30 September; September DRAM contract settlements.

Skyworks jumps 13.6% as the Qorvo merger clears down to two jurisdictions

What happened

Skyworks was Tuesday's standout chip move, closing at $90.00, up 13.55% (close, 15 Sept). CEO Phil Brace said the $22bn Qorvo merger has "entered its final regulatory phases, with approvals now pending in only two remaining jurisdictions," targeting a close within the calendar year and roughly $500m in cost and revenue synergies; Apple's first foldable iPhone, announced at the line's top price point, is the second catalyst, since Skyworks supplies RF front-end parts into that lineup. Qorvo rose 9.34% on the fixed exchange ratio (Investing.com).

What it means

RF has been the least glamorous corner of semiconductors and the one most exposed to a single customer. Consolidating the two largest Apple RF suppliers into one is a bet that scale beats Apple's ability to play them off each other — a bet that only pays if the foldable actually sells units.

Business

The 10-year closed at 5.00% — the first close at or above 5% since August 2007

What happened

On the eve of the FOMC decision the long end finished at a level it has not touched at a close in nineteen years. Treasury's official curve for 15 September: 2-year 4.67%, 10-year 5.00%, 30-year 5.36%, against 4.65/4.97/5.34 on Monday (Treasury daily yield curve). Note the precision: 5.00 is the first close at or above 5% since 20 August 2007 — not above it — and the October 2023 scare peaked at 4.98%. Schwab put the hike probability at 92% on the CME FedWatch tool. Kevin Gordon of the Schwab Center: "When we think about the main drivers of inflation—tariffs, energy, and AI capex—none have slowed materially" (Schwab, 15 Sept).

What it means

The target range is 3.50–3.75%, set on 29 July, and the market is not arguing about direction any more — only about the 2027 dot. A hike into an energy shock is the Volcker trade, and the bond market has already decided the Fed will take it. Schwab's Collin Martin offers the other reading: "Treasury yields might not be a problem that needs to be fixed. They are indicative of the economic environment we're in."

The decision and the dot plot today at 2pm ET; the BOJ on 17–18 September; August PCE on 26 September.

Oil back above $100 on both benchmarks — and Washington says the Saudi pipeline is days from restarting

What happened

Crude ran hard Tuesday: WTI +2.64% to $104.10 and Brent +2.14% to $107.90, both front-month intraday quotes rather than settlements; Schwab had WTI at $102.88 earlier in the session, a gap of timing rather than dispute. Against that, US Energy Secretary Chris Wright said Saudi Arabia's East–West pipeline should be back online on a timeframe that is "still a detailed assessment, but I think it will be measured in days," speaking at a G20 energy meeting in Houston (Al-Monitor, 15 Sept). The line had been carrying 4–5m barrels a day in recent months as shippers routed around a closed Strait of Hormuz — roughly 4–5% of global supply, against a nameplate capacity near 7m.

What it means

That 4–5m figure is emergency volume, not normal throughput, which is what makes the restart date load-bearing: with Hormuz shut since March the pipeline is not a secondary route, it is the route. And "days" is an American official's estimate of a Saudi repair; no Aramco statement has put a number on the damage.

Startups

Profound raises $180m at $1.8bn, seven months after its last round, for getting brands into AI answers

What happened

Profound, two years old, closed a $180m Series D at a $1.8bn valuation co-led by Sequoia and Kleiner Perkins, with Lightspeed, Khosla Ventures and South Park Commons joining. Its $96m Series C closed less than seven months ago. The company sells answer-engine optimisation — software that tracks and shapes how a brand appears inside AI-generated search answers — and reports 3x revenue growth over six months and more than 1,000 enterprise customers, naming Comcast, Estée Lauder and Walmart (TechCrunch, 15 Sept).

What it means

Seven months between a $96m round and a $180m round at unicorn pricing is investors underwriting a category rather than a company. The bet is that AEO becomes a permanent marketing line item the way SEO did. The risk is identical to SEO's: the platforms can make the whole discipline obsolete in a single ranking change, and here the platforms are also the labs.

US

Crypto market-structure legislation dies in the Senate, 49–50

What happened

Cloture on the motion to proceed to H.R. 3633 — the CLARITY Act, which would split digital-asset oversight between the SEC and CFTC — was rejected 49 to 50 on roll call 234 at 2:19pm Tuesday (Senate roll call 234). Sixty were needed; it did not clear fifty. Four Republicans crossed — Collins, Hawley, Moran and Tillis — and not one Democrat or independent voted yes. The break came over a conflict-of-interest title. Warren called the Republican "final offer" on ethics "a weak fig leaf that will do nothing to stop him from making his next $1.4 billion in crypto profits"; SEC chairman Paul Atkins said that "with or without that legislation, this Administration will deliver for American investors and technological innovators" (Axios). Bitcoin fell to under $76,000, Coinbase more than 8%, Strategy 5%.

What it means

Three years of lobbying failed on a margin no whip operation can close, because the objection is not to market structure — it is to writing rules that touch the President's own crypto holdings. Atkins' line is the plan: regulate by SEC rulemaking instead, which is faster, narrower and reversible by the next administration.

Three hours later, the same Senate advanced the college-athlete bill 74–24

What happened

At 5:00pm Tuesday, roll call 235 invoked cloture on S. 4668, the student-athlete name, image and likeness bill, 74 to 24 (Senate roll call 235). Earlier that day roll call 233 confirmed Matthew Byrne to the Southern District of Ohio, 52–45. Separately, the Lindsey Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act reaches the House floor this week and is splitting Democrats: it would let the administration impose tariffs of up to 100% on the five largest buyers of Russian oil. Meeks, Beyer and Neal, jointly: "This bill would dramatically expand presidential tariff authorities while failing to mandate sanctions on Russia." It passed the Senate 86–11 last month.

What it means

Seventy-four votes for college sports and forty-nine for crypto, in the same afternoon, says this Senate can still legislate — just not on anything touching the President's business interests. The Graham bill puts House Democrats in the reverse bind: the sanctions they want, delivered through the tariff power they have spent two years litigating.

CBO puts the Iran war at $38bn and adds half a point to its own 2027 inflation forecast

What happened

The Congressional Budget Office published its cost estimate for the Iran campaign on 15 September: "As of August 1, 2026, the armed conflict with Iran has cost DoD approximately $38 billion" (CBO, publication 62756). The run rate is conditional: about $2bn a month at May–June intensity, $3bn at July intensity. CBO now puts year-over-year PCE inflation in the first quarter of 2027 half a percentage point higher than it projected in February — a revision against its own forecast, not a standalone claim. Rebuilding the interceptor inventory "would probably take at least five years."

What it means

The cost of the war stated by the scorekeeper rather than by either party, landing the morning of a Fed decision. Half a point on the 2027 forecast is roughly the difference between one more hike and two. The interceptor line is the more serious number: a five-year magazine-depth problem constrains every other commitment, including Taiwan.

Watch next

Whether the estimate surfaces in Fed commentary this afternoon; the supplemental; FY27 munitions lines.

World Politics

Ukraine claims ground at Lyman as Russia runs 200 drones overnight

What happened

ISW's 15 September assessment has Ukrainian forces advancing in the Lyman direction and against the Russian salient northwest of it, with the 3rd Army Corps striking Russian logistics and fuel under what Kyiv calls Operation Vivaldi — enough, ISW judges, to disrupt Russia's wider encirclement of the Fortress Belt. Russia launched 200 drones overnight; Ukraine struck Russian oil and defence-industrial targets. Zelensky again said he would accept a mutual ceasefire on energy infrastructure. In the Baltic, a Russian frigate fired flares at a Danish helicopter on routine surveillance, and ISW notes European sabotage attempts on 14–15 September matching Russian methods while stating plainly that Russian involvement is unconfirmed (Kyiv Post, 15 Sept). Operational claims here are Ukraine's own account, relayed by ISW, and are not independently verified.

What it means

Lyman is the first place in months where Ukraine is shaping the map rather than absorbing pressure, and it is doing it by striking fuel and logistics rather than by mass. The Baltic incidents are the other half of the same campaign — pressure applied where NATO's response threshold is undefined.

Merz books a post-election showdown with his own party for Sunday night
