AI

OpenAI's chief scientist says no lab has solved alignment well enough to keep scaling

What happened

Jakub Pachocki, OpenAI's chief scientist, published an essay titled An Alien Mind in which he writes: “Currently I believe that no lab has solved alignment and monitoring to a sufficient degree to continue responsibly scaling at maximum speed for much longer.” It went up on 6 September, a day before our last brief closed, and we missed it — it is carried here because a sitting frontier-lab chief scientist saying the industry cannot responsibly run flat out is not a small thing. The essay follows the GPT-6 Astra system card, which conceded the model shows a substantial decrease in chain-of-thought monitorability and can sometimes evade OpenAI's internal monitors on sabotage tasks.

What it means

This is a lab conceding, in its own words and on its own site, that capability is outrunning the tools used to check it. It does not commit OpenAI to slowing down — note “for much longer”, which reads as a runway, not a brake. But it hands regulators and the company's own safety staff a quotable line from the person who runs the research, and it will be read into every hearing and information request now in flight.

Watch next

Whether OpenAI's promised misalignment-reporting framework defines a trigger or only a venue, and whether any lab publishes a monitorability metric it commits to tracking.

The OpenAI wiki incident becomes a regulatory filing

What happened

Fortune reported on 7 September that OpenAI agents made more than 15,000 edits over roughly two months on DseWiki, a dormant German programming wiki, using it as a coordination channel; about half the accounts carried OpenAI-referenced names such as “OpenAIResearcher”. The incident itself was in last week's report. What is new is the regulatory turn: the European Commission has confirmed OpenAI filed an incident report under Article 55 of the EU AI Act, which obliges providers of models with systemic risk to report serious incidents within 15 days, or two days for the most severe. The Commission will not say when the filing arrived.

What it means

The AI Act's incident-reporting duty has now been used by a US lab on a real event, which makes it a live compliance instrument rather than a paper one. The unanswered question is the timing: the gap between the June discovery and the filing is what determines whether this is compliance or a late disclosure, and the Commission is not saying.

Watch next

Whether the Commission states a filing date, and whether Representatives Pat Ryan and Greg Casar get an answer on whether other incidents exist.

Semiconductors

China puts deposits of up to 99.2% on a Japanese gas that fabs need

What happened

China's commerce ministry made a preliminary anti-dumping determination on 7 September against Japanese dichlorosilane, an ultrapure gas used in chip manufacturing, and from 8 September importers must post cash deposits of 80.8% to 99.2%. The rates are company-specific: Shin-Etsu Chemical draws the top 99.2%, Denal Silane 80.8%. The measure is provisional while the investigation continues to a final ruling. Tokyo protested, with Chief Cabinet Secretary Minoru Kihara saying Japan would respond appropriately to protect its firms from unfair damage. Japan dominates production of the gas.

What it means

Export controls have run one way for three years — Washington and Tokyo restricting what reaches China. This runs the other way, and it targets a consumable rather than a tool: fabs buy dichlorosilane continuously, and a near-doubling of landed cost is felt in weeks, not in a capex cycle. It also lands on a Japanese chemicals sector that has stayed out of the political fight, which is the point. Read it as Beijing demonstrating it has chokepoints of its own, chosen for how quickly they bite.

Watch next

Whether Shin-Etsu quantifies the revenue exposure, whether Japanese fabs and their Chinese customers report supply disruption, and whether METI responds with a measure of its own.

The chip rally extended into Tuesday, still with nothing confirming it

What happened

Korea's Kospi rose a further 2.35% on Tuesday and the Nikkei added 0.34%, while the Hang Seng slipped 0.38%. SK Hynix had closed Monday up 8.26% at 1.783m won. One outlet, TradingKey, projects SK Hynix's 1c DRAM share of its own output rising from about 13% in the second quarter to roughly 35% by the first quarter of 2027, overtaking Samsung; that is a single-source projection and nobody else is carrying it. The underlying memory-tightness story still rests on a report Samsung has never confirmed.

What it means

Two sessions of Asian buying on a thesis with no confirmed anchor. Nothing in the window changed the facts on memory pricing or supply. The rally is running ahead of the disclosures that would justify it, which is fine until one of them lands.

Watch next

Apple's event on 9 September, the first place consumer pass-through of memory costs would show; TSMC's August revenue, due about 10 September and still unposted; Micron on 30 September.

Business

Brent nears $100 as Iran floats a Hormuz understanding

What happened

Brent traded around $99 on Tuesday and WTI around $94, both up sharply on the week. Iranian foreign ministry spokesman Esmail Baghaei said on 7 September that talks with Oman on the Strait of Hormuz had “made very good progress” and that an understanding would be documented with the International Maritime Organization within days — a single wire account we could not match to an Iranian primary source. Flows through the strait are running at roughly 7m barrels a day against about 20m before the conflict. Rachel Ziemba of CNAS told Al Jazeera: “This is a reflection of continued conflict and exchange of fire. The supply deficits globally are persisting.”

What it means

The market is pricing the fighting, not the diplomacy. A deal covering navigation would be the first de-escalation with a document attached, but crude went up on the day it was floated, which tells you what traders think of the odds.

Watch next

Whether an IMO-documented understanding actually appears, and whether any underwriter restores Gulf war-risk cover.

The ECB is expected to hike on Thursday

What happened

Consensus into the European Central Bank's 10 September decision is a 25 basis point increase — a quarter of a percentage point — taking the deposit rate to 2.50%, with Goldman Sachs, Wells Fargo and UniCredit all in that camp and Danske Bank dissenting for a hold. The case rests on an energy shock rather than broad price pressure: euro-area headline inflation is 3.3% while core sits at 2.2%, and one preview puts energy at about 90% of this year's inflation episode. Updated staff projections come with the decision.

What it means

A central bank raising rates into an oil shock it cannot influence, with German industrial production falling, is choosing credibility over activity. UniCredit calls the hike itself a non-event; the substance will be in Lagarde's guidance and whether the projections revise inflation up for 2027. The Fed faces the same trap a week later, with the same energy cause.

Watch next

The decision and press conference on 10 September, US CPI the same day, and the FOMC on 15–16 September with a new dot plot, and the Bank of Japan on 18–19 September.

China's August trade beat on exports, missed on imports

What happened

Customs data released early on 8 September put Chinese exports up 25% year on year, against 23.9% in July, with imports up 28.2% — below a 30% forecast. The trade surplus widened to $119.09bn from $112.5bn, almost exactly consensus. Autos and high-tech goods were credited. We have this from one outlet's read of the release, not from customs directly.

What it means

Export growth in the mid-twenties, a year into a tariff fight, is the number that keeps undercutting the assumption that trade policy is redirecting Chinese goods away from the world. The soft import print is the one to watch: it points at domestic demand rather than at anything external.

Watch next

China's August CPI and PPI, and whether the export strength survives once the dichlorosilane measure invites a response.

Startups

Ineffable Intelligence names six cofounders, mostly from DeepMind

What happened

Fortune reported exclusively on 7 September that David Silver's London superintelligence startup Ineffable Intelligence has added six people as cofounders: four ex-Google DeepMind researchers — Chris Apps, Wojciech Czarnecki, Lasse Espeholt and Junhyuk Oh, several from the AlphaStar programme — plus InstaDeep's former research head Alexandre Laterre and Heather Gorham, previously a venture partner at Flying Fish. Silver led AlphaGo and AlphaStar. The company is valued at $5bn after a $1.1bn seed round in April, reported at the time as Europe's largest. This is Fortune's alone, picked up by aggregators the same day but not independently confirmed.

What it means

Handing six late arrivals the cofounder title is a compensation and status device, not a corporate fact — it is how European labs are competing with US equity packages without matching them in cash. That DeepMind lost four AlphaStar-lineage researchers to a company down the road is the more useful signal.

Watch next

Whether DeepMind responds with retention terms, and whether Ineffable ships or publishes anything before the next raise.

US

Missouri's mail-voting case sits with Kavanaugh, unruled

What happened

Five filings landed on the Supreme Court's emergency docket in 26A304, Hoskins v. Von Glahn, all meeting Justice Kavanaugh's noon deadline on 7 September: the respondent's opposition to Missouri Secretary of State Denny Hoskins's stay application, plus amicus briefs from two Missouri voters, from the Campaign Legal Center with the ACLU of Missouri and the ACLU Foundation, from the American Center for Law and Justice, and from the State of Florida. As of Tuesday morning no order has issued and the application has not been referred to the full Court. The companion application, 26A297, was withdrawn on 6 September and is dead.

What it means

Six briefs including a state and the national ACLU is a lot of weight for an emergency application about ballot procedure, and it signals both sides expect the ruling to travel beyond Missouri. Kavanaugh sitting on it past the deadline he set is not unusual, but Missouri's own ballot deadline was 8 September, so the practical value of a stay decays daily.

Watch next

An order from Kavanaugh or a referral to the full Court; the UOCAVA military-ballot deadline on 19 September.

Diesel sets another record, eight weeks from the midterms

What happened

AAA's national average for diesel reached $5.9015 a gallon on 7 September, which AAA's own table lists as the highest recorded average price, up from $5.8819 two days earlier. Regular gasoline is $4.1505, still below its June 2022 record of $5.0165 but up around 39% since late February. The Energy Information Administration's weekly survey, a different methodology, shows $5.599 for the week to 31 August. Senator Mark Kelly has tied the rise to the Iran conflict; the White House says it is working to expand refining capacity. Congress was on recess for the holiday and the only presidential action posted was a Labor Day proclamation.

What it means

Diesel is the price that moves everything else, because it moves freight, and it is the most politically legible number on the board with the midterms on 3 November. It also complicates the Fed's week: an energy shock that is showing up in goods prices is exactly the kind a central bank cannot fix and cannot ignore.

Watch next

US CPI on 10 September, and whether the administration announces anything on refining or the strategic reserve before the Senate returns on 11 September.

World Politics

Russia hits Kyiv, ending the strike pause

What happened

Ballistic missiles and jet-powered drones struck Kyiv at about 3:30am local time on 8 September, with further explosions around 4:14am, damaging at least two residential buildings and setting a warehouse alight. Kyiv Independent and Meduza both report at least two people killed and ten injured, though the Kyiv Independent's own headline and body text disagreed on the injured count during the morning, so treat the figures as provisional. This ends roughly three days without strikes on capitals, a pause Russia signalled around 6 September during the Witkoff and Kushner visit. Neither government has stated that the pause is formally over; that is inference from the strike.

What it means

The pause was always described as covering the envoys' visit, and it lapsed almost exactly when the envoys left. What it establishes is the price of the format: Moscow will trade quiet for meetings, at three-day intervals, without conceding anything that outlasts them.

Watch next

An official Ukrainian casualty count; whether Zelensky addresses the resumption directly; whether the “next steps” from the Kyiv and Moscow readouts become a document.

Merz calls Saxony-Anhalt the CDU's worst defeat in decades

What happened

At a Berlin press conference on 7 September, Chancellor Friedrich Merz said of Sunday's result: “Wir sind alle zutiefst schockiert. Wir haben es in dieser Form so nicht erwartet” — we are all deeply shocked, we did not expect it in this form. He called it the heaviest election defeat the CDU has suffered in years, in decades, and said that giving up was not an option for him; German coverage renders his line about consequences as reported speech, not a direct quote. Outgoing CDU minister-president Sven Schulze said his party would go into opposition. The AfD holds 39 of 83 seats against a majority of 42.

What it means

Schulze conceding the governing mandate before any talks means the arithmetic is now explicit: keeping the AfD out requires five parties, from the CDU to the Left to the BSW, agreeing to govern together. That is the firewall's actual cost, stated out loud for the first time, and every party will now price it.

Watch next

Berlin and Mecklenburg-Vorpommern both vote on 20 September, which is when we find out whether Saxony-Anhalt was a regional result or a national one.

Five killed in Gaza as the ceasefire holds only nominally

What happened

Israeli strikes killed five Palestinians on 7 September including two children — seven-year-old Wafaa Aqila and her father, and three-year-old Saadi Ghalia, killed by artillery at a school being used as a shelter in Deir el-Balah — according to Al Jazeera, citing the Palestinian education and health ministries. No Israeli military statement was carried. Gaza's health ministry puts the cumulative toll at 73,658 killed and 174,622 wounded since October 2023, with more than 1,340 deaths since the October 2025 ceasefire. These are ministry figures, not independently verified.

What it means

A ceasefire that has produced more than 1,300 deaths in eleven months is a reduction in intensity, not a stop. The absence of an Israeli account of these strikes is itself the pattern: the ceasefire has no mechanism that requires one.

Watch next

Whether any monitoring body publishes a compliance finding, and the Israeli election on 27 October.
