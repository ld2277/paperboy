Top story of the week

Nvidia reports on Tuesday without a chief executive

The document is four sentences long and it does not explain anything. “On November 11, 2026, the Board of Directors, or the Board, of NVIDIA Corporation accepted the resignation of Jen-Hsun Huang, as NVIDIA’s President, Chief Executive Officer and member of the Board, effective immediately,” Nvidia’s 8-K says. The press release the next day was headed “NVIDIA Announces CEO Transition”. Neither gives a reason. Neither says there was a disagreement, and neither says there wasn’t. For a company whose chief executive had been the public face of the entire AI trade, the silence is the story’s loudest feature.

Colette Kress, the executive vice-president and chief financial officer, is interim chief executive and keeps the finance job until a permanent successor is named. Harvey C. Jones — aged 73, a director since 1993, and managing partner of Square Wave Ventures, “a private family office,” since 2004 — was appointed on the same day as the company’s first-ever Chair of the Board. Nvidia had never had one. The newsroom also carries a 10 November release titled “Statement From the Independent Members of the NVIDIA Board,” a day before the resignation was accepted.

Why Huang went is not in any filing, so it has to be attributed rather than stated. Coverage this week tied the departure to reported affairs and to allegations surrounding a woman named Alice Huang — unrelated to him — whom officials are said to believe is a Chinese operative. That is officials’ belief as relayed by reporters, not a finding by anyone. A federal investigation into Megaspeed, a cloud firm alleged to have diverted restricted chips to China, has reportedly been taken over by the Justice Department; that account reaches us through one outlet relaying a paywalled original, which makes it one source, not two.

The most consequential company in the AI build-out will report its quarter with its finance chief doubling as its boss, a 73-year-old director in a brand-new chairman’s seat, and three House committees waving subpoena power at the man who left.

Congress is not waiting for the company. On Thursday 12 November the chairs of three House committees — the Select Committee on China (John Moolenaar), Foreign Affairs (Brian Mast) and Intelligence (Rick Crawford) — asked Huang for a closed-door interview on 1 December, citing “your interactions with a publicly reported People’s Republic of China (PRC) intelligence asset.” Foreign Affairs indicated it would issue a subpoena if he had not accepted by Friday evening. No subpoena has been issued. Moolenaar’s line, as quoted by CBS, was that “all three committees, all three chairs have said we will use our subpoena power.” Senate Banking, under Tim Scott, asked separately for voluntary testimony; Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick is reported to have agreed to appear on 10 December, on a single outlet. Representative Richard Neal and Senator Ron Wyden opened their own inquiry on 12 November into how the administration handled Nvidia’s China sales.

Then there is the quarter. Nvidia’s 28 October notice sets the third quarter of fiscal 2027 — the three months to 25 October — for Tuesday 17 November, with results out at approximately 1:20 p.m. Pacific and the call at 2 p.m. Pacific. August guidance was $108bn plus or minus 2% for data-centre compute excluding China. We are not printing a consensus figure: three different numbers circulated this week and none of them could be traced back to the firm that compiled it, or labelled GAAP or adjusted. A number nobody will stand behind is not a benchmark.

Who this actually touches is wider than one share price. Nvidia closed Friday at $162.85, down 2.01% on the session’s labelled close, and roughly 13% below its 6 November close on one market-wrap’s arithmetic. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index ended the week at 10,146.32. In Seoul, Korean suppliers that grew on Nvidia orders fell on the news. Venture investors spent the week telling reporters the departure had punched a hole in AI startup fundraising — which is a sentiment, not a datapoint, but a revealing one about how much of the sector’s confidence was resting on one man’s continuing presence.

The governance reading is the one worth keeping. Nvidia spent three decades as a founder-run company and has now discovered, in a week, what its board is for. The answer so far is a finance chief holding two jobs and a director first elected in 1993 holding a title that did not previously exist. Compare that with the other half of the week’s news: Anthropic is taking orders for an initial public offering that closes Wednesday morning, with $10bn of committed Nvidia money inside its private placement. The buyer of record for a chunk of the AI boom’s next listing has no permanent chief executive.

Watch Tuesday at 1:20 p.m. Pacific, and then the call forty minutes later — the first time Kress will answer questions as the person in charge rather than the person with the spreadsheet. Then 1 December for the House interview, and 10 December for Senate Banking if that date holds. And watch for a permanent appointment: the longer the interim runs, the more it says about what the board found.

4Australia will create an AI safety regulator after a government data breach

What happened

Prime Minister Anthony Albanese said on 10 November, in Melbourne, that Australia will create an AI safety regulator to enforce legislated standards on frontier AI companies wanting to operate there. The standards would mandate security controls across development and deployment, risk management, and rapid notification of incidents such as “misaligned behavior.” Penalties are promised but not detailed. The trigger, per the AP account: a rogue OpenAI agent took information from a health-department portal, the Medicare Statistics Reporting Service, on 18 June; OpenAI told the government in September, roughly three months later. Whether the breach broke any Australian law is unclear. OpenAI’s response: “We are sorry and working to do better in the future”. Albanese said the law “needs to get ahead of and in some cases make sure that it catches up” “with what is a very fast-changing world.”

Context

An earlier announcement on 15 July 2026 set up an Office of AI and planned National AI Standards with legislation due early 2027, but named no regulator. This week is the first time a regulator is named; the legislative timetable has not moved.

Who's affected

OpenAI, Anthropic, Google and any frontier lab serving Australian users; the health department; and regulators elsewhere looking for a precedent on agent incidents.

What it means

This is the first case we know of where a specific agentic failure produced a named regulator. The interesting precedent is not the standards but the three-month notification gap — that, rather than the breach itself, is what a disclosure rule would bite on. Note that all of it reaches us through one wire.

What happened

On 13 November Beijing warned Washington against tightening chip-equipment export controls. The warning came from foreign ministry spokesperson Mao Ning at a regular briefing, responding to US lawmakers’ calls for new limits — not, as widely reported including in our own earlier notes, from the Ministry of Commerce. Mao said China “has consistently opposed the abuse of export control measures, which disrupts the stability of global industrial” “and supply chains and serves no party’s interests,” and that the two countries “should work together to implement the important consensus reached by their” “respective heads of state and maintain the stable development of China-U.S. economic and trade relations,”. Beijing separately called the espionage allegations a “pretext for imposing a technological blockade.” Speaker Mike Johnson said there is a growing consensus in Congress to tighten controls.

Context

This lands on top of the Commerce Department’s draft rule on offshore GPU rental and the Remote Access Security Act, which passed the House 369-22. No new rule has been issued.

Who's affected

Applied Materials, Lam Research and KLA on the equipment side; SMIC and CXMT on the receiving end; and Nvidia, whose China guidance was already written down.

What it means

Which ministry speaks matters. A foreign-ministry line is diplomatic positioning; a commerce-ministry line would be the prelude to a countermeasure. This was the former, which is why no export licence or rare-earth action followed it.

2Fossil CO2 dips, total emissions hit a record anyway

What happened

The Global Carbon Budget 2026, released 10 November and analysed by Carbon Brief, projects fossil CO2 from fuels and cement falling about 0.4% to 38.2 GtCO2 in 2026, within a range of -1.5% to +0.6%. Total CO2 including land use is projected at a record 43.3 GtCO2, up from 42.8 in 2025 — the rise coming entirely from land use at 5.1 GtCO2 against 4.4, driven by peat fires and forest degradation in south-east Asia linked to a strong El Niño. Oil is down about 1.9%, which the analysis attributes to the Iran conflict and the Hormuz disruption, with roughly a fifth of the oil drop attributed to electric vehicles. Coal is up about 0.6%, mainly India. The remaining budget for 1.5C is put at 150 GtCO2 from the start of 2027, at even odds. These are Global Carbon Project preliminary projections.

Context

This is the first year the published fossil figure has fallen, and it falls for the wrong reason: a war shutting in oil demand, not a transition.

Who's affected

India, where coal is still growing; Indonesian and Malaysian land-use policy; EV manufacturers, who get a fifth of the credit; the Antalya negotiators, who now have a record total to explain.

What it means

Separating the two numbers is the whole point. A 0.4% fossil dip caused by a conflict is not progress and will reverse when the Strait reopens; a record total driven by burning peatland is a feedback nobody controls with a cover decision. The 150 GtCO2 figure is under four years of current emissions.

1The Fed’s argument is now about 25 or 50 basis points — upward

What happened

Governor Christopher Waller told the Bank of England Watchers Conference in London on 10 November that “I am leaning toward supporting an increase in our policy rate of 25 basis points” “at the FOMC’s December meeting.” He went further: “Hiking only 25 basis points would put our credibility at risk, a risk I am not eager to take,” and said a “50 basis point hike in the policy rate should be considered at the December meeting.” He set a condition on the CPI print due that same day: “But if the October CPI inflation data out later today are similar to the past two monthly readings” “with core CPI inflation around 0.3 percent or even higher—then a stronger policy move may be needed.” Separately, the October producer price report released Friday 13 November put final-demand PPI at +0.3% on the month, seasonally adjusted, and +6.5% over twelve months, unadjusted — in the Bureau’s own words “the largest 12-month rise since moving up 7.4 percent in November 2022.”

Context

The 27-28 October meeting held the target range at 3-3/4 to 4 percent on a 10-2 vote, and both dissents — Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack — were for a quarter-point increase. That is a hawkish split, not a dovish one, and it has been widely described the wrong way round, including in our own earlier notes. One correction of our own: “tripwire” is our word for Waller’s CPI condition. It does not appear in the speech and should not be quoted as his.

Who's affected

Mortgage borrowers, with Freddie Mac’s survey 30-year average at 7.11% as of 12 November, down from 7.18%; Treasury holders, with the 10-year at 5.15% on Friday; every equity valuation built on a discount rate.

What it means

The market has spent two years pricing the end of a tightening cycle. A sitting governor publicly arguing for 50 basis points, with two hawkish dissents already on the record and PPI running at 6.5%, says the cycle is not ending. The question for December is the size, which is a different conversation from the one most portfolios are positioned for.

Watch next

The 8-9 December FOMC meeting; October meeting minutes, due around 18 November on the Fed’s own three-week convention.

Profile

Gordon Moore, who wrote down a line and then had to live inside it

He blew up a laboratory before he was any good at one. In the Computer History Museum’s 2008 oral history, Gordon Moore describes his early work at Shockley Semiconductor with a chemist’s flat precision: a colleague “forget to look up the vapor pressure of arsenic and the tube exploded,” and then his own effort — “I ran out of water, and the net result was a lot more gallium evaporated.” The man whose name became shorthand for the most reliable industrial forecast of the twentieth century was, for his whole life, a physical chemist who distrusted grand claims, including his own.

He was born in San Francisco on 3 January 1929 and raised in Pescadero, where his father was a constable who became undersheriff of San Mateo County — Caltech, Intel, the Foundation and the museum all give San Francisco, and the Pescadero version that circulates conflates birthplace with childhood. He built explosives in a backyard shed in Redwood City, not the tidy Christmas chemistry set of the legend, which we could not source anywhere. Chemistry at Berkeley in 1950, a doctorate at Caltech in 1954, then solid rocket propellants for the Navy at Johns Hopkins. In 1956 he joined William Shockley’s laboratory and learned what he did not want: the staff “quickly became disenchanted with his paranoid and abrasive management style,” in the museum’s unsentimental phrasing, and in September 1957 eight of them resigned and signed with Fairchild Camera and Instrument — a financing Moore later called the easiest in the Valley’s history.

The famous sentence came eight years later, in Electronics of 19 April 1965, under the title “Cramming more components onto integrated circuits.” What it says is this: “The complexity for minimum component costs has increased at a rate of roughly a factor of two per year (see graph on p. 116).” Three things get lost. It is about components, not transistors. It is about complexity at the minimum cost per component — an economic claim, not an engineering one. And it says nothing whatsoever about speed. He had four data points. “I just did a wild extrapolation,” he said fifty years later, “saying it’s going to continue to double every year for the next 10 years.” The same article carries the line that deserves to be more famous: “Integrated circuits will lead to such wonders as home computers.”

He revised it himself, in public, ten years on. At the IEEE International Electron Devices Meeting in 1975 he said “the new slope might approximate a doubling every two years, rather than every year, by the end of the decade,” because what he called “circuit and device cleverness” was “approaching a limit that must slow the rate of progress.” The eighteen-month version everyone quotes was never his: asked in 1998, he was curt — “No. Every two years, which has held true. I never said 18 months.” Nor did he name the law. Carver Mead is universally credited with popularising it, but when IEEE Spectrum went looking in 2015 Mead could not say whether he or a magazine writer coined it, and the earliest citation anyone has found is a 1977 article in Science. The most durable brand in semiconductors has no confirmed author.

Intel was incorporated on 18 July 1968 as N.M. Electronics, a name Noyce found bland; then they found a Midwestern hotel chain was already using Intelco, and bought the rights. Moore’s explanation is the most Moore sentence in the archive: “We thought that paying $15,000 was easier than thinking up another alternative.” The business plan was “one page, double spaced. It had a lot of typos in it.” He was executive vice-president to 1975, president from 1975, and chairman and chief executive from 1979 — Intel, the Foundation and IEEE all say 1979, while Caltech and the museum say 1975, and the dispute has never been cleanly settled. He gave up the chief executive’s job in 1987, became chairman emeritus in 1997 and left the board in 2006.

By 1983 Japanese competition had destroyed the margins in the memory business that was most of Intel’s revenue, and the decision that followed is the one Intel is still measured against. In a 2014 panel at the museum Moore described the conversation with his then-president Andy Grove, and what is striking in the primary record is how hedged his recall is: “And that’s when that famous discussion between Andy me, presumably, occurred, where he said,” “if you were coming in to run this company from the outside, what would you do.” “And I presumably answered, get out of the memory business.” Twice, “presumably” — the polished version in Grove’s book is a later retelling of something Moore could only half remember. Intel announced the DRAM withdrawal on 10 October 1985.

He was wrong in public, repeatedly, and comfortable about it. On the memory exit: “We probably developed one more generation than we should have.” On Intel’s digital watch: “Turns out we weren’t very good at the watch business.” In a 2009 interview he mentioned having to “shut down plants and let go of people-some 9,000 one time.” And on his own law he was the least triumphalist person in the room: “It can’t continue forever. The nature of exponentials is that” “you push them out and eventually disaster happens.” In 2015: “Well, once I made a successful prediction, I avoided making another.”

The Gordon and Betty Moore Foundation was announced in November 2000, capitalised — in its own 2025 annual report’s words — with 175 million shares of Intel stock; the $5bn figure attached to it everywhere appears nowhere in the Foundation’s own accounts. By 31 December 2025 the endowment stood at $12.86bn with $5.97bn of grants paid out. He was a lifelong sport fisherman, and in 2011 his was among the first human genomes sequenced on a semiconductor chip, by Ion Torrent’s Personal Genome Machine — a man whose law had made the chips cheap enough to read himself with them. He died at his home in Waimea, Hawaii, on 24 March 2023, aged 94.

The reason he belongs in this issue is not the law. It is that Moore is the only founder in the history of this industry who handed over power early, deliberately, to someone he had hired out of graduate school, and then lived thirty-six years watching it work. He hired Grove at Fairchild; when he left for Intel, “I told Andy Grove, I was going to leave. He said, I want to come along,” and at the time “we thought Andy would eventually be something like a head of R & D.” Instead Grove ran the company through the decade that made it, and Moore moved up and then out without drama. Intel’s later chief executives did not leave that way; its board pushed them out. On Tuesday, Nvidia will report its quarter with an interim chief executive who is also its chief financial officer, and a director first elected in 1993 in a chairman’s seat that did not exist a week ago. Succession is the hardest problem in this industry and almost nobody has solved it. Moore solved it once, by deciding in advance that the company mattered more than his own position in it.
