Top story of the week

The compute boom reaches the price tag on a $55 computer

Korea's Ministry of Trade, Industry and Resources published its September trade figures on 1 October, and they are the kind of numbers that make you check the decimal point. Total exports came to $120.94bn, up 83.5% on September 2025 and the first month in the country's history above $120bn. Semiconductors alone accounted for $60.3bn of it, up 262.8% year on year and, by the ministry's own account, the first time chip exports have cleared $60bn in a single month. Imports rose 26.0% to $71.09bn, leaving a trade surplus of $49.85bn. Shipments to China rose 122.7% to $26.01bn and to the United States 137.0% to $24.33bn. The one category that shrank was cars, down 5.5% to $6.05bn.

Those are not normal numbers for a mature industrial economy, and they are not a volume story. Micron's fourth-quarter results, reported at the end of September, show where the money is coming from. In the company's own segment table, the Mobile and Client unit - the memory that goes into phones, laptops and desktop PCs - earned an 88% operating margin on $13.114bn of revenue. That is Micron's most profitable business, ahead of Core Data Center at 85%, Automotive and Embedded at 79% and Cloud Memory at 76%. Company gross margin was 86.8% on a GAAP basis. Mobile and Client revenue grew 14% quarter on quarter, and the prepared remarks say exactly why: "driven by higher pricing, partially offset by lower bit shipments". It was the one unit that sold less memory and earned the most money doing it.

The mechanism is not mysterious. Memory capacity is fungible over years and fixed over months, and for two years the industry has been redirecting it toward high-bandwidth memory for AI accelerators, where the margins are better and the buyers are desperate. Everything else - the DRAM in a laptop, the LPDDR in a phone, the modest 2GB module in a single-board computer - now competes for leftover capacity against a bidder with effectively unlimited willingness to pay. Chief executive Sanjay Mehrotra told analysts that more than 75% of Micron's output is already committed for 2027. The prepared remarks go further: even with additional industry cleanroom plans and new upside requests from customers, "we do not have line of sight to when supply and demand will return to balance".

Which brings us to the clearest illustration of the week. On 1 October, Raspberry Pi raised the price of the 2GB Raspberry Pi 4 from $55.00 to $67.50 and the 2GB Pi 5 from $65.00 to $77.50, both by $12.50, effective the same day. The 1GB variants and the 2GB compute modules were left alone. It is the second memory-driven rise this year - a February post had already added between $10 and $60 across the range. The company's language is unusually direct for a corporate price announcement: "The cost of memory has risen very steeply over the past two years and continues to increase." And then: "This has now become unsustainable, and from today we are increasing the price of the 2GB variants by $12.50." The closing note is almost wistful - the price of memory "will eventually subside, and we look forward to reversing our own price increases when it does".

Raspberry Pi has now raised prices twice in eight months, and it is not because demand for hobby computers has surged.

Who is affected depends on where you stand in the queue. Micron, SK hynix and Samsung are the winners, and Korea's trade balance with them. The losers are everyone buying memory as an input rather than selling it: PC and phone makers whose bill of materials moves against them every quarter, embedded and industrial customers who buy in small volumes and have no leverage, and schools and hobbyists for whom $12.50 on a $55 board is a 23% increase. The deeper exposure is to companies that treated cheap commodity memory as a permanent condition of the world. That assumption held for most of three decades. It does not hold now.

Is this a cycle or a regime? The people best placed to know are declining to say, and Micron's "no line of sight" language is a supplier refusing to forecast its own market - which is itself information. The supply response is real and slow. The glass-substrate packaging that would relieve one bottleneck has just slipped: a TrendForce survey on 30 September has BOE in customer validation with a mass-production investment decision reported for the first half of 2027 and full output no earlier than 2028, Samsung Electro-Mechanics targeting 2027 and LG Innotek 2028. None of that puts an additional DRAM die into a Raspberry Pi before 2027.

So the regime case is the stronger one for the next eighteen months, and it has a political dimension nobody has priced. For twenty years consumer electronics got cheaper in real terms every year, and that was one of the quiet reasons goods inflation stayed tame. If AI capital spending is now bidding up the memory in every device, that disinflationary contribution disappears - and it disappears exactly as central banks fight energy-driven inflation. Christine Lagarde told the European Parliament last week that firms will put around 10% of total investment into AI in 2026 and that AI-related borrowing already accounts for roughly a quarter of credit growth to companies. That is the same phenomenon seen from the financing side. The memory bill is what it looks like from the shop floor.

What to watch, with dates. TSMC publishes September monthly revenue around 10 October. Samsung's preliminary third-quarter guidance is due in the first half of the month and SK hynix reports later; look for allocation language - how much capacity each commits to high-bandwidth memory versus commodity DRAM for 2027. Korea's October trade figures land on 1 November and will show whether a $60bn chip month was a peak or a new floor. The Korean exchange is closed on 5 and 9 October and Taiwan on 9 October, so the Asian tape will be thin until mid-month. And watch the second derivative in consumer hardware: whether a phone or laptop maker is next to put a memory surcharge in writing, as Raspberry Pi has now done twice.

AI

1AMD buys Fei-Fei Li's World Labs for $8.2bn in stock

What happened

AMD announced on 28 September that it will acquire World Labs, the spatial-intelligence and world-models startup, in an all-stock transaction valued at approximately $8.2bn, with Fei-Fei Li joining as executive vice president and chief scientist reporting to chief executive Lisa Su. The deal is expected to close by the end of 2026, subject to regulatory approval. AMD's 8-K filed the same day confirms the consideration is paid in common stock, with the share count to be fixed on a ten-day volume-weighted average price ending two trading days before closing - so the final dilution is genuinely unknown today and depends on where AMD trades between now and the close.

Context

Li built ImageNet, the dataset that made modern computer vision possible, ran Stanford's AI lab and co-founded World Labs in 2024 to pursue models that understand three-dimensional space rather than text. AMD has spent the last two years closing the gap with Nvidia on accelerators and crossed a $1tn valuation last month. Buying a research lab rather than a product company is a different kind of acquisition for a semiconductor firm.

Who's affected

AMD shareholders, who are paying in equity at a high valuation. Nvidia, which now faces a competitor with an in-house frontier research voice. Robotics and autonomous-systems companies, for whom world models are the missing layer. And Stanford's AI ecosystem, which loses its most prominent figure to a chipmaker.

What it means

Su's own quoted reasoning is the tell: "Building the compute platforms for the next generation of AI requires a deep understanding of how models are evolving." That is an argument that hardware roadmaps can no longer be set from the outside - that you have to be in the model business to know what silicon to build in 2029. It is also, less charitably, $8.2bn of stock spent on a signalling exercise at a moment when AMD's paper is expensive. Both readings are available. What is not available is a revenue case: AMD disclosed none, and World Labs has no meaningful product revenue.

Watch next

Whether an S-4 or proxy filing discloses the exchange ratio and dilution. Regulatory clearances through Q4. And whether Nvidia responds with a research acquisition of its own.

2Anthropic puts $100m into training 10,000 deployment engineers

What happened

Anthropic announced the Claude Frontier Academy on 2 October: $100m to train 10,000 "Frontier Deployed Engineers" by the end of 2027, explicitly modelled on medical residency. Participants take a multi-day in-person course with Anthropic engineers covering simulated enterprise deployments and security review, earn a Claude Resident Engineer badge, then lead a twelve-week residency on a real project inside their own organisation to earn the full credential. The first full graduates are expected in early 2027. Launch partners are Accenture, Bain, Capgemini, Commonwealth Bank of Australia, Deloitte, McKinsey, Morgan Stanley and Novo Nordisk, with programmes in San Francisco, New York and London. Anthropic says 175,000 Claude certifications have been earned across 46,000 firms and that Commonwealth Bank teams have produced up to three times more code changes in the past year.

Context

The consistent complaint from enterprise buyers of frontier models for two years has been that the model works in a demo and fails in production, because nobody inside the company knows how to wire it into real systems. Consultancies have been filling that gap at consultancy rates. This is the model vendor deciding to manufacture the supply itself.

Who's affected

The eight launch partners, who get a credential their competitors do not have. System integrators, whose scarcity rent on deployment expertise is the thing being attacked. And the engineers themselves, for whom a vendor-issued credential becomes a labour-market asset - the cost to participants was not disclosed.

What it means

This is a distribution play dressed as education, and it is the right one. The binding constraint on enterprise AI revenue is not model capability, it is the number of people who can deploy it; $100m to create 10,000 of them is cheap against the revenue they unlock. The risk is the one every vendor certification runs: a credential controlled by the seller is worth whatever the seller's market position is worth, and no more.

Watch next

Whether the first cohort actually produces 10,000 credential-holders on schedule, and whether Microsoft or OpenAI launch competing programmes. Consumer and enterprise revenue disclosure in Q4 is the real scoreboard.

3OpenAI and Synopsys put an agent in the chip-design loop

What happened

The two companies announced "GPT-Synopsys" on 30 September, a multi-year agreement with revenue sharing under which OpenAI licenses Synopsys design-automation tools and the two run joint research and go-to-market. The model is hosted on OpenAI infrastructure and integrated with Synopsys.ai and Synopsys Autopilot, so that engineers delegate design objectives - power, performance, area, timing, verification - to agents that drive the tools and iterate. No financial terms and no launch date were disclosed; early engagements are under way with unnamed semiconductor customers.

Context

Electronic design automation is one of the few software markets with genuine moats: three vendors, decades of accumulated process knowledge, and customers who cannot switch. It is also unusually well suited to agents, because the objective function is numerical and the verification step is automated.

Who's affected

Cadence and Siemens EDA, who now have to answer this. Chip design teams, whose headcount economics change if a junior engineer's iteration loop can be delegated. And OpenAI, which gets a foothold in the industry that supplies its own compute.

What it means

The revenue-sharing structure is the interesting part. Synopsys is not buying a model licence; it is giving up a share of future revenue in exchange for not having to build frontier capability itself. If that becomes the template, frontier labs end up with a royalty on the design of the chips they run on, which is an unusually good place to sit. Note also Greg Brockman's framing - "We're using our most advanced technology to improve the systems that power AI" - which is a recursive-improvement argument stated in a press release.

Watch next

Any disclosure of the revenue-share terms in Synopsys filings. A Cadence response. And the first tape-out that credits an agent.

2Micron's most profitable business is the memory in your phone

What happened

Micron's fiscal fourth-quarter release discloses segment operating margins of 88% for Mobile and Client on $13.114bn of revenue, 85% for Core Data Center, 79% for Automotive and Embedded and 76% for Cloud Memory. GAAP gross margin was 86.8% (non-GAAP 87.0%). Mobile and Client revenue grew 14% quarter on quarter "driven by higher pricing, partially offset by lower bit shipments". Chief executive Sanjay Mehrotra told the earnings call that more than 75% of output is already committed for 2027, and the prepared remarks say the company does "not have line of sight to when supply and demand will return to balance".

Context

These are segment figures Micron published itself, not an outside estimate - worth saying, because an 88% operating margin on a commodity is the kind of number that normally turns out to be someone's arithmetic. For NAND specifically the company expects the industry to remain supply constrained through both 2027 and 2028.

Who's affected

Phone and PC manufacturers, whose bill of materials now moves against them quarterly. Micron's own customers negotiating 2027 contracts against a supplier with three-quarters of its output already sold. And anyone modelling consumer electronics pricing on three decades of falling memory costs.

What it means

An 88% operating margin in a commodity business is not a sign of a healthy market. It is a sign of a market where the clearing price is set by a bidder - AI infrastructure - who is indifferent to the price. The forward-commitment figure is the one to carry: when 75% of next year's output is sold before the year starts, spot prices stop being a signal and become a tax on whoever did not pre-commit.

3Raspberry Pi raises prices for the second time this year

What happened

Effective 1 October, the 2GB Raspberry Pi 4 went from $55.00 to $67.50 and the 2GB Pi 5 from $65.00 to $77.50, both up $12.50. The 1GB variants of both boards and the 2GB Compute Module 4 and 5 are unchanged. A February 2026 post had already added $10 to $60 across the range depending on capacity. The company says the cost of memory "has risen very steeply over the past two years and continues to increase", that "this has now become unsustainable", and that it looks forward to reversing the increases when memory prices subside.

Context

Raspberry Pi buys DRAM in volumes that are rounding errors to Micron and Samsung, which is exactly why it is a useful instrument: it has no pricing power, no long-term supply agreements worth the name, and no ability to absorb input costs. It prices what it is charged.

Who's affected

Education buyers and makers, for whom $12.50 on a $55 board is a 23% increase. Industrial and embedded customers designing around the 2GB SKUs. And every small hardware company whose 2026 budget assumed stable component costs.

What it means

This is the most legible consumer-facing consequence of AI capex that exists right now. Two increases in eight months, with the company explicitly naming memory and explicitly declining to pretend it is anything else, is better evidence of a supply regime than any analyst note. Note that the 1GB SKUs were protected both times - the company is deliberately preserving an entry price point and taking the margin hit elsewhere.

Watch next

Whether a third increase arrives before the end of the year, and whether a larger consumer hardware maker follows with an explicit memory surcharge.

New Technologies

1Google puts four TPUs in orbit

What happened

NPR reported on 1 October that Google's Project Suncatcher prototype reached orbit on a SpaceX launch aboard a satellite operated by Planet Labs: one fridge-sized satellite carrying four TPUs, on a mission planned to run about a year. Two more satellites launch next year to test laser interconnection between craft. Technical detail published 28 September describes demonstrated optical transceiver performance of 800 Gbps per direction and a target of tens of terabits per second between satellites, with TPUs running in fifteen-minute bursts before thermal shutdown and launch loads of up to 10g sustained.

Context

The argument for orbital compute is power: continuous solar with no night, no grid interconnect queue and no local objection to a data centre. The argument against is everything else - cooling in vacuum, radiation, launch mass and the fact that you cannot send a technician.

Who's affected

Planet Labs, which gets a marquee hosted payload. Terrestrial data-centre developers facing moratoriums and ratepayer fights. And in the long run, the economics of where inference happens.

What it means

Worth noting how cautious Google's own people are. Project lead Travis Beals says "it's going to take a long time, it's not going to be a completely straightforward journey" and, more pointedly, "I don't see this being cheaper in the next five years". A senior director publicly declining to claim cost advantage is the most informative line in the coverage. Read this as a physics experiment with a long option value attached, not a data-centre strategy. The fifteen-minute thermal duty cycle is the detail that tells you how far off this is.

Watch next

Results from the one-year mission. The two-satellite laser-link test next year. And whether any competitor files for a constellation licence.

2The FDA approves the first heart valve designed to grow with a child

What happened

The FDA granted premarket approval - its most stringent device pathway - to the Autus Size-Adjustable Valve on 2 October, for Edwards Lifesciences. The surgically implanted pulmonary valve is expanded after implantation by balloon catheter as the child grows, from roughly 13mm to 22mm, and uses synthetic polymeric leaflets rather than animal-derived tissue.

Context

Children who need a pulmonary valve today face a series of open-heart operations as they outgrow each one. Every avoided reoperation is a thoracotomy a child does not have. The clinical history of paediatric valves is a history of repeat surgery.

Who's affected

Children with congenital pulmonary valve disease and their families. Paediatric cardiac surgeons. Edwards Lifesciences, which adds a first-in-class device.

What it means

The approval pathway matters as much as the device: premarket approval, not the 510(k) clearance route that most devices take, means the FDA required clinical evidence of safety and effectiveness rather than equivalence to something already on the market. For a device implanted in a child and then repeatedly expanded, that is the right bar. We have deliberately left out the enrolment and adverse-event figures circulating in coverage - they are not in the company release and we could not reach the FDA's summary of safety and effectiveness data to check them.

Watch next

The published SSED for the pivotal trial data. Long-term durability of polymeric leaflets, where there is no decade-scale evidence. And reimbursement coding.

Climate Change

1Switzerland lost more than 5% of its glacier ice this year, and two glaciers entirely

What happened

GLAMOS, the Swiss glacier monitoring network, reported through the Swiss Academy of Sciences on 1 October that Switzerland lost more than 5% of total glacier volume in 2026, with average thickness down 2.5 to 4 metres and some tongues losing up to 10 metres. Nearly 20% of the country's entire glacier volume has disappeared in the five years 2022-2026. Bella Tola in Valais and Griessfirn in Glarus were declared completely and permanently gone. Winter 2025-26 was among the ten least snowy on record, and the freezing level sat above 4,000 metres for 76 days - more than twice the average and a Swiss record. Swiss glaciers released roughly 2,200 billion litres of water between July and September.

Context

Alpine glaciers are the most closely measured ice on earth, with a continuous Swiss record stretching back over a century, which is why these figures carry weight that a model projection does not. 2022 remains the record melt year overall.

Who's affected

Downstream water users across the Rhone and Rhine basins who depend on glacial melt for summer flow. Swiss hydropower. Alpine tourism and mountain communities. And, with a longer lag, everyone sharing a coastline.

What it means

The nuance in the report is more alarming than the headline, not less. Overall melt rates did not quite reach 2022's record - but the Rhone, Aletsch and Clariden glaciers individually had their worst melt years on record in 2026. That pattern, where the aggregate is slightly below the record while the largest individual glaciers set new ones, is what accelerating loss in the biggest ice masses looks like. Losing a fifth of national glacier volume in five years is not a trend you extrapolate; it is an endgame you count down.

Watch next

The 2027 winter snowpack, which determines next summer's baseline. The next GLAMOS annual report in autumn 2027. And whether Swiss water-allocation policy changes in response.

2The Senate kills the data-centre ratepayer bill three votes short

What happened

On 30 September the Senate rejected cloture on the motion to proceed to H.R. 9340, the Ratepayer Protection Act, by 57 to 43 - three short of the 60 needed. The House had passed it 417 to 3 on 16 September. The bill would have required state regulators to consider shifting grid infrastructure costs onto data centres and other large electricity users rather than households. Four Democrats - Hassan, Klobuchar, Ossoff and Warnock - voted to advance it; most Democrats opposed it as too weak, arguing the requirement to merely "consider" cost-shifting had no teeth.

Context

Roughly 5,400 data centres operate in the United States, and PJM's total wholesale power cost rose 76% year on year in the first quarter. A September poll found 53% of Americans extremely or very concerned about data centres' effect on electricity prices or water supply. More than 100 local moratoriums are pending.

Who's affected

Household ratepayers in data-centre corridors - Virginia, Ohio, Georgia, Texas. Hyperscalers, who keep the status quo. State public utility commissions, which now have no federal direction. And the four Democrats who will be asked about this vote.

What it means

A bill that passes the House 417-3 and dies in the Senate on a party-line-plus-four cloture vote is not a bill anyone actually opposed on the merits. It died because Democrats judged a toothless bill worse than no bill, betting they can get a stronger one later. That is a defensible bet and a risky one: the ratepayer anger is real and currently unmatched by any federal remedy, which pushes the fight entirely into state legislatures and local zoning. Note for the record that no state ratepayer-protection bill advanced during this week - Oklahoma's passed in May.

Watch next

Whether Democrats introduce the stronger alternative. State legislative sessions opening in January. And the next PJM capacity auction.

3A federal judge halts the Big Bend border wall and stays the environmental waivers

What happened

On 2 October, Judge Kathleen Cardone of the Western District of Texas issued a preliminary injunction halting border barrier construction across the Big Bend sector - more than 500 miles of West Texas including Big Bend National Park, Big Bend Ranch State Park and Rio Grande frontage. The order also stays DHS's waivers of the National Environmental Policy Act, the Archaeological and Historic Preservation Act and dozens of other laws. Plaintiffs are the Center for Biological Diversity, Friends of the Ruidosa Church and river guide Danny Miller Jr. Cardone found plaintiffs likely to succeed on at least one claim and likely to suffer irreparable harm. The Justice Department said it is working on next steps.

Context

The waiver authority, not the construction money, has been the operative mechanism for border barrier building since 2017: a single DHS determination switches off environmental review for a stretch of border. Staying the waivers rather than just the bulldozers is the part that has precedential weight.

Who's affected

Big Bend's ecosystems and the species that cross the river. Landowners and river outfitters along the affected stretch. Contractors with mobilised crews. And DHS's legal strategy nationwide.

What it means

Note the evidentiary detail the plaintiffs used: the Big Bend sector ranks last of nine US border sectors for unauthorised crossings. That is how you beat a national-security justification - not by disputing the goal but by showing the chosen location does not serve it. Whether this is a temporary restraining order or a full preliminary injunction is described differently across outlets; the docket would settle it, and we have not read it.

Watch next

A Fifth Circuit appeal, where the administration has generally fared better. The written order's treatment of the waiver authority. And whether other sectors see copycat suits.

1The jobs report and the factory survey cannot both be right

What happened

Friday's September employment report put non-farm payrolls at just 29,000 against expectations near 84,000, with July revised to minus 10,000 and August to 133,000 - a combined 60,000 of downward revisions - and unemployment at 4.2%. The day before, S and P Global's final US Manufacturing PMI rose to 55.9 from 53.9, the highest since May 2022, with manufacturing employment rising at the greatest rate in over five years and supplier delivery times lengthening the most since August 2022. The ISM manufacturing index read 54.5. Chris Williamson of S and P Global: "September has seen the pace of US manufacturing growth pick up a gear again, the PMI lifting to its highest since May 2022 as a surge in new orders encouraged factories to lift output sharply higher."

Context

Other data from the week pulls the same way as the surveys rather than the payroll print: second-quarter GDP was revised up to 2.2% annualised from 1.5%, real final sales to private domestic purchasers rose 4.6%, business investment grew 7.7% against consumer spending at 3.8%, and ADP reported private employers adding 90,000 jobs in September. Against that, real disposable income stalled in August, real wage growth fell for a fifth consecutive month, and the personal savings rate dropped to 4.1% from 5.2% a year earlier.

Who's affected

The Federal Reserve, which has to choose which series to believe. Manufacturing workers, who appear in one dataset and not the other. And anyone whose job depends on the establishment survey being the true read.

What it means

Do not resolve this; the divergence is the information. The most coherent story that fits both is an economy where output and investment are strong, concentrated in capital-intensive sectors, and hiring is not - which is what AI-driven capex looks like in the national accounts. A manufacturing survey reporting the strongest factory hiring in five years while the establishment survey reports 29,000 jobs across the whole economy also tells you the weakness is somewhere other than factories. Worth noting the survey detail: delivery times lengthened on stock shortages, shipping and customs delays, notably at the Canadian border.

2An October rate rise went from near-certain to a long shot in one morning

What happened

Before Friday's jobs report, markets had the October FOMC priced at roughly a 70% probability of a rate rise. After it, that collapsed. Prediction-market composites put the odds at around 18% as of 3 October, with a separate model-tracking page giving 19.6% as of the 2 October close and roughly 80% on hold; one dated market report put it at about 25%. Context

This is a Fed that has been tightening into a slowing labour market, which is an unusual posture and one it has justified on inflation grounds. The September decision was unanimous. Who's affected

Borrowers at the short end. Banks' net interest margins. Equity valuations, which rallied on the news. And the committee's own credibility, which is now exposed either way - hiking into visible labour weakness, or not hiking after saying inflation required it.

What it means

Be careful with the number: the figures in circulation are prediction-market composites, not CME-sourced federal funds futures, and we could not reach CME FedWatch for a clean read. Call it roughly one in five and name the source type. The substantive point is that the Fed's reaction function is about to be tested in public. If it holds in October after a 12-0 hike in September, the September decision looks like a mistake it is quietly correcting. If it hikes anyway, it is tightening into a labour market that generated 29,000 jobs in a month.

3The Paramount-Warner deal clears its last hurdle and gets a new name

What happened

Three things happened in three days. On 30 September, US District Judge Araceli Martinez-Olguin approved the settlement resolving the antitrust suit brought by California leading twelve states against the $110bn Paramount Skydance-Warner Bros. Profile

Paul Volcker: the man who proved a central bank could be hated and right

The week produced a jobs report showing 29,000 new positions, revisions wiping out 60,000 more, and a Federal Reserve that raised rates unanimously a fortnight earlier and may raise them again this month. The long end of the Treasury curve touched levels last seen in 2002. If you want to know what it looks like when a central bank decides inflation matters more than employment and then absorbs what the country does to it in return, there is exactly one modern case study, and he was six feet seven inches tall and smoked twenty-eight-cent cigars.

Paul Adolph Volcker Jr. was born in Cape May, New Jersey, on 5 September 1927 and raised in Teaneck, where his father was the township manager. The formative family story was fiscal: during the Depression, Volcker senior cut his own $8,000 salary by $2,000. The son went to Princeton and graduated summa cum laude in 1949 - not, as is often said, as valedictorian; the valedictorian in the family was his mother, at Vassar. His senior thesis was titled "The Problems of Federal Reserve Policy Since World War II". He was twenty-one and had already chosen his subject. A Harvard master's followed in 1951, then a year at the London School of Economics on a Rotary fellowship.

His career alternated between the New York Fed, Chase Manhattan and the Treasury: economist at the New York Fed from 1952, Chase in 1957, Treasury in 1962, Chase again in 1965, Treasury in 1969 as Under Secretary for Monetary Affairs. That last post put him in the room for the most consequential monetary decision of the century's second half. In August 1971, at Camp David, Volcker pressed Nixon for what he called a "cold-blooded suspension" of the dollar's convertibility into gold. He meant it as temporary leverage over the Europeans. It ended Bretton Woods permanently, and the inflation that followed became the problem he spent two decades fixing. He helped build the thing he later had to dismantle.

He became president of the Federal Reserve Bank of New York in 1975 and, on 6 August 1979, Jimmy Carter's chairman of the Federal Reserve Board. Inflation was running toward 14%. The Fed's existing method - targeting the federal funds rate within a narrow band and adjusting gently - had failed for years, because every time rates bit, the political cost arrived before the disinflation did and the Fed blinked.

On Saturday 6 October 1979, two months into the job, Volcker convened an unscheduled meeting of the Federal Open Market Committee and held a rare evening press conference. The announcement sounds technical and was not: the Fed would stop targeting the funds rate and start targeting bank reserves, accepting whatever interest rate resulted. The operating band for the funds rate went from fifty basis points in September to four hundred in October - 11.5% to 15.5%. Translated: the committee was removing its own ability to flinch. It could no longer quietly decline to raise rates, because it was no longer setting them. The rate became an output.

What came out was brutal. The effective federal funds rate peaked at 19.10% in June 1981; the posted prime rate reached 21.5% in December 1980. Consumer price inflation, which hit 14.8% year on year in March 1980, was down to around 3% by 1983 - the fastest disinflation in American peacetime history. It cost two recessions and unemployment of 10.8% in November and December 1982, the highest since the Great Depression. Farmers drove tractors to Washington and encircled the Fed's building. Homebuilders mailed in bricks and sawn two-by-four planks with postage stamps stuck to them. Car dealers mailed in the keys to cars they could not sell. There were calls for his impeachment, and a man with a sawn-off shotgun got close to the boardroom before security stopped him.

Here is the detail worth reading aloud to whoever is nearby. Throughout all of it, Volcker earned $57,500 a year as chairman of the Federal Reserve, having made $110,000 at Chase. He lived alone in a spartan apartment in a building full of George Washington University students while his wife stayed in New York; his daughter helped with his laundry. He chain-smoked Antonio y Cleopatra Grenadiers, which cost twenty-eight cents each, through meetings where he was setting the price of money for the entire world. The man who made mortgages cost 18% was taking home less than a mid-level banker and buying the cheapest cigars on the shelf.

He got things wrong, and the most instructive error is the least remembered. In March 1980 the Fed imposed credit controls and they backfired spectacularly: Americans cut up their credit cards and mailed them in as a patriotic gesture, collapsing consumer borrowing and the money supply far faster than intended and deepening the 1980 recession. Volcker treated it afterwards as a lesson in regulatory humility. The 1982 Mexican default was the bill arriving somewhere else - when finance minister Silva Herzog announced in August that Mexico could not service roughly $80bn of debt, Volcker structured a $600m swap and convened central bankers for an emergency bridge loan with the Bank of England and the Swiss National Bank. High dollar rates had made Latin American debt unpayable, and the man who raised them spent that autumn stopping the consequence from taking down the American banking system.

He left the Fed on 11 August 1987, succeeded by Alan Greenspan, and spent thirty years as the man called when an institution needed someone whose integrity nobody would question: Arthur Andersen's independent oversight board in 2002, the UN inquiry into the Iraq oil-for-food programme in 2004-05 with Richard Goldstone and Mark Pieth, and the Volcker Rule in the 2010 Dodd-Frank Act - a ban on banks trading for their own account that left Congress narrower than he proposed and was narrowed again in 2019. He died on 8 December 2019, aged 92.

The reason he matters this month is not the 19% funds rate. It is the institutional question he answered. Volcker's insight in October 1979 was that the Fed's credibility problem was structural rather than analytical: everyone knew the committee would stop before the job was done, because it always had, and so inflation expectations never fell. His solution was to take the discretion away from himself. Today's Fed has a target range, a unanimous September vote, a labour market that produced 29,000 jobs in a month, and markets that have just repriced October from near-certain to roughly one-in-five. Whichever way it goes, it will be a discretionary choice made by people who can be talked out of it. Volcker's answer to that was to build a system in which he could not be - and then to stand there while the country mailed him its car keys.
