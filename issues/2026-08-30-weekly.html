Top story of the week

Compute is revenue. Memory is the bill.

On Wednesday evening Nvidia reported the largest quarter any company has ever had selling a single category of hardware. Revenue of $96.2bn, up 106% on the year. Data-centre revenue of $89.0bn. A gross margin of 75.0%. Guidance for the current quarter of $108.0bn, give or take 2%. Jensen Huang's line in the release — “AI has reached its inflection point” — was the sort of thing chief executives say when the numbers already say it for them. The stock closed up 8.7% the next day, its best session since April 2025.

One number in the filing did more work than the headline. Nvidia's purchase commitments have gone from $119bn to $279bn, and the company says the increase is primarily the procurement of memory. That is not a company buying growth. That is a company locking up a scarce input before someone else does, at whatever the price turns out to be. Two days earlier TrendForce had published the arithmetic behind that instinct: DRAM and NAND, which took 47% of cloud operators' hardware spending in 2026, will take 68% of it in 2027. Cloud capital expenditure is rising 98% this year and another 50% next. Server DRAM prices are up roughly 270% year-on-year through the end of 2026. Enterprise SSDs, 235%.

The supply side explains why none of this resolves quickly. At the Hot Chips conference in Cupertino, Micron's HBM design architecture fellow Raghu Sreeramaneni laid out the physics. High-bandwidth memory consumes about three times the wafer area of DDR5 for the same capacity and sells for about five times as much per bit, so every gigabyte that goes into an accelerator removes disproportionate capacity from the commodity market. An HBM4 die carries 256 banks against 32 on a DDR5 die. In a two-GPU package, memory is now roughly 90% of the silicon — about eight times the area of the processors it feeds. His verdict: “the memory wall is still present, and, in fact, maybe getting worse.”

“AI has reached its inflection point. It's doing useful work. Its tokens are productive and profitable. Now, compute is revenue.” — Jensen Huang

So the cost lands somewhere, and it is starting to land on people who have never bought an accelerator. The Register, reporting the TrendForce numbers, notes PC prices climbing by double digits, PC shipments down 5%, smartphone shipments expected to fall 15% this year, and the French hosting company OVHcloud warning customers of increases of “up to 87 percent.” Nvidia is reported to be raising prices on its Vera Rubin and Grace Blackwell systems by 15%, though that figure is press reporting rather than anything Nvidia has published. Korea's tax receipts tell the same story from the profit side: Samsung and SK hynix together paid 11.2 trillion won — about $8.1bn — in corporate tax in the first half, up 167% on the year, with SK hynix alone paying 7.22 trillion of it.

Money is arriving to meet the demand, and increasingly it is not equity. Lambda, which rents Nvidia hardware to Microsoft, closed a $926m senior secured term loan on 27 August and, per Bloomberg, is raising another $1bn in short-dated private debt arranged by JPMorgan — its third borrowing of the year. Bloomberg's own tally puts AI-related debt raised globally in 2026 at over $400bn. Andreessen Horowitz, a firm built on software margins, announced a $1.1bn fund on Friday called Machine Age, aimed at chips, memory, cooling, electrical infrastructure and real estate. When the venture industry's most software-native shop starts buying transformers and land, the constraint has moved.

Which brings it to the only institution obliged to care about all of this at once. On Thursday, in his hundredth day as chairman, Kevin Warsh told Jackson Hole that the twelve-month change in the PCE price index stands at 3.7% and the six-month change at 4.1%, and that “the responsibility for 65 months of sustained, elevated inflation sits squarely with the central bank. And that is where it belongs.” He is not obviously wrong about the past. The harder question is the present one: a large, rate-insensitive, debt-financed capital programme is bidding up a globally traded input, and the pass-through is visible in laptops, phones and hosting bills. That is not the kind of inflation a policy rate reaches quickly. It may be the kind that keeps a central bank from cutting for a long time.

Who this actually touches, concretely: anyone buying a PC or a phone in the next eighteen months; Dell, HP and Lenovo, who buy memory and sell to people who notice prices — HP's fiscal third quarter already credited “meaningful improvements in memory supply” for its beat, with unit volumes down 16%; the neoclouds, whose business model assumes they can deploy hardware fast enough to service short-dated debt; and the Korean duopoly, which is having the best two years in its history and has told anyone who asks that the shortage runs through 2030. Best Buy's quarter on Thursday — comparable sales up 4.1%, guidance raised — suggests the American consumer has not flinched yet. The Conference Board's expectations index, at 68.2 and falling, suggests they can see something coming.

What to watch, with dates. Dell reports fiscal Q2 on Tuesday 1 September and will be the first large buyer to talk about memory costs since the last contract settlements. September DRAM contract prices settle over the next few weeks, and Micron and SK hynix will have to say publicly how much consumer allocation they are sacrificing to HBM. The FOMC meets on 15–16 September, a meeting that carries a Summary of Economic Projections — from which Warsh personally abstains. Three of his colleagues already voted to raise rates in July. If the September PCE print does not come down, the argument the Fed is having about the past becomes an argument about the next twelve months, and this build-out is squarely inside it.

What happened

Sony Music Publishing (US) LLC and Warner Chappell filed suit on Friday 28 August in the Northern District of California, San Jose division, case number 5:26-cv-09217. The defendants are Anthropic PBC and, unusually, its founders Dario Amodei and Benjamin Mann as individuals. The prayer for relief asks up to $150,000 per work for wilful infringement plus up to $25,000 for each removal of copyright-management information — the “hundreds of thousands per work” figure circulating in coverage is a compression of those two stacked claims, not a number in the complaint.

Context

Anthropic settled the Bartz class action for $1.5bn — agreed in September 2025, finally approved on 21 July 2026 — which remains the largest copyright settlement in US history. The same legal team filed for Concord and Universal in January 2026. Music publishers have been the most aggressive rightsholders in the AI copyright fight, partly because compositions are individually registered and therefore easy to enumerate for statutory damages.

What it means

Statutory damages are what make this dangerous. Tens of thousands of works at a $150,000 ceiling is arithmetic nobody wants to do out loud, and it is precisely the arithmetic that produced the $1.5bn Bartz number. The individual-defendant theory is the novel part and the part most likely to be tested first.

Watch next

A motion to dismiss the claims against Amodei and Mann personally, which is close to certain. Which judge draws the case — Bartz was William Alsup's throughout, and a repeat draw would matter. Any on-the-record statement from Anthropic, which has said nothing.

2The EU AI Act's teeth come out, and the AI Office can now demand access to models

What happened

The AI Act's transparency and disclosure obligations became binding on 2 August 2026, and the first serious assessments of what actually changed landed this week. Chatbots and AI-generated content now carry disclosure duties across the bloc, and the European AI Office holds a standing power to request information from companies and to demand access to their models — the first regulator anywhere with that authority over frontier systems. Anthropic, Google, Meta, Microsoft and OpenAI have each announced compliance measures: watermarking, transparency tooling, training-data summaries. The next tranches, covering high-risk uses in education, biometrics, migration and physical products, land in December 2027 and August 2028.

Context

The Act was agreed in 2024 with a staged commencement precisely so that industry could build to it. The August 2026 date is the one that turns a statute into an enforcement regime. Practitioners have spent two years arguing that the text is unworkable; they are now arguing it in front of a regulator that can ask to see the weights.

What it means

Both are right, which is the point. Brussels has again set the global default by moving first, as it did with GDPR, and American labs are complying in Europe rather than withdrawing from it. The model-access power is the genuinely new thing: it converts AI safety from a voluntary disclosure regime into an inspectable one.

Watch next

The first formal information request from the AI Office and whether a lab contests it. Whether any penalty figures are published — no fine has yet been levied under the Act.

4An Anthropic researcher shows an automated alignment researcher beating humans, at $4 an hour

What happened

Anthropic fellow Chen Yueh-Han published a paper on Friday 28 August, “Automated Researchers Can Reliably Mitigate Alignment Failures,” describing a system that searches the literature, proposes alignment methods and trains models in 30-minute iterations with no human in the loop. Context

5Anthropic beats the Pentagon in court — and the ruling vacates, it does not merely enjoin

What happened

On Friday 28 August, Judge Rita Lin of the Northern District of California issued a 59-page final order on cross-motions for summary judgment vacating the Defense Department's supply-chain risk designation of Anthropic. This is a merits judgment, not a preliminary injunction — a distinction most coverage got wrong. Lin found the penalties were imposed “based on a desire to make a public example out of Anthropic for its ‘arrogance' in criticizing the government, not based on any articulable basis to believe that Anthropic would actually sabotage its model.” An earlier preliminary injunction in March 2026 was a prior stage of the same case.

Context

The designation followed public criticism of the administration by Anthropic. A parallel case remains pending in the DC Circuit. The government generally has 60 days to file a notice of appeal from a final judgment.

What it means

The finding of retaliatory motive is what gives the ruling reach. An agency that loses on procedure can redo the procedure; an agency found to have acted to punish a critic cannot simply re-run the process with better paperwork.

Watch next

A notice of appeal to the Ninth Circuit — none filed as of Sunday. Any Pentagon statement; it has declined to comment. Whether another vendor invokes Lin's reasoning.

3Micron at Hot Chips: “the memory wall is still present, and, in fact, maybe getting worse”

What happened

Raghu Sreeramaneni, Micron's HBM design architecture fellow, gave the supply-side explanation at Hot Chips 2026. HBM takes roughly three times the wafer area of DDR5 for equivalent capacity and prices at about five times DDR5 per bit. An HBM4 die carries 256 banks against 32 on DDR5; a single HBM3E die does 256 GB/s against roughly 8 GB/s for a DDR5 die; HBM4 runs 2,048 host-interface I/Os above 11 Gb/s per pin. In a two-GPU package, memory is now about 90% of the silicon and roughly eight times the GPU die area. He described HBM as “probably the most cross-functionally complex solution that we make,” said the gap is “definitely not getting better,” and on thermals described “architecting solutions around thermals rather than the other way around.”

Context

This is the technical reason the price story does not resolve with a new fab. Wafer capacity converted to HBM is capacity removed from the commodity market at a 3:1 ratio, and the ratio worsens each generation. HBM4E, due around 2027, moves the logic base die to TSMC — a further tightening, since it puts memory in competition with logic for leading-edge capacity.

What it means

It is worth separating two claims. That memory is scarce is a market fact that could reverse. That HBM structurally consumes disproportionate silicon is a physics fact that will not. The second is why forecasters keep being wrong in the same direction.

Watch next

HBM4E qualification timelines. Whether TSMC's base-die role for HBM4E shows up as a constraint in its capex guidance. Hot Chips slides are not published, so this rests on trade-press accounts — treat the specifics as single-sourced.

5The Korean memory supercycle turns up in the tax receipts

What happened

Samsung Electronics and SK hynix together paid 11.2 trillion won — about $8.1bn — in corporate tax in the first half of 2026, up 167% year-on-year, split 3.99 trillion for Samsung and 7.22 trillion for SK hynix. The figures come from the companies' own regulatory filings via Korea's DART repository, reported by Yonhap and independently by the Korea Herald. Context

The striking detail is that SK hynix paid roughly 1.8 times Samsung's bill despite being much the smaller company. What it means

A duopoly earning like this has every incentive to hold discipline on supply rather than build its way back to commodity margins. That is the mechanism by which the shortage persists — not physics alone, but physics plus a rational oligopoly.

1NASA's Roman Space Telescope launches on a Falcon Heavy

What happened

The Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope lifted off at 7:26am EDT on Sunday 30 August from Launch Complex 39A at Kennedy Space Center, on a SpaceX Falcon Heavy — a centre core and two side boosters, 27 Merlin engines and more than five million pounds of thrust. Side-booster separation came about two minutes after maximum dynamic pressure. Roman begins a five-year primary mission to map billions of galaxies, detect exoplanets and study black holes.

Context

Roman is the wide-field companion to the James Webb Space Telescope: where Webb stares deep at small patches, Roman surveys enormous areas fast. It has survived several rounds of proposed cancellation to get here.

What it means

NASA's framing is that Roman will “provide vast volumes of daily data.” That is the actual change: survey astronomy becomes a data-processing discipline before it becomes a discovery one, and the bottleneck moves from telescope time to pipeline capacity.

Watch next

Commissioning and first-light images over the coming months. NASA's launch blog carries no mission cost or instrument specification, so treat any figure circulating elsewhere with care.

Climate Change

1Global sea surface temperature hits an all-time record of 21.1°C — in August

What happened

Copernicus announced on 24 August that the daily global average sea surface temperature reached 21.1°C on 22 August, breaking the 21.09°C record set in March 2024. The measurement comes from the ERA5 reanalysis, which runs back to 1979. Samantha Burgess, strategic lead for climate at ECMWF: “This record is another clear signal of an ocean under growing stress. El Niño is adding heat to the system, but it is doing so on top of decades of human-driven warming.”

Context

The timing is what makes it unusual. The previous record was set in March, when the ocean average peaks; August is a month when the global mean should be falling. A strong El Niño is developing in the tropical Pacific and the WMO projects it could reach levels unseen for several decades.

What it means

Ocean heat is the least noisy climate indicator there is — it integrates over the whole system and does not bounce around with weather. A record set out of season is therefore harder to dismiss as variability than an equivalent air-temperature record.

Watch next

The September Copernicus monthly bulletin, and whether the El Niño strengthens into the northern winter.

2Glaciologists on the Himalayan collapse: a glacier snout came off at 5,200 metres

What happened

The 26 August glacier collapse into the Lende river in Nepal's Rasuwa district, and across the border in Tibet's Gyirong county, drew unusually specific comment from glaciologists. Vikram Gupta of Sikkim University confirmed that “a substantial portion of that glacier snout collapsed.” Dan Shugar of the University of Calgary: “The lower part of a glacier broke off at about 5,200 metres.” Dorothy Heinrich of the University of Reading: “We do know what climate change trends look like in the Himalayas.” On the warning problem, hydrologist Hatim Sharif of UT San Antonio said the speed of the event “defeats essentially every operational flood warning system.”

Context

Hindu Kush Himalaya glaciers lost ice 65% faster in 2011–2020 than in the preceding decade, and Nepal's glaciers have lost roughly a third of their ice volume in about thirty years, on UN figures from 2023. The scientists quoted are explicit that single-event attribution cannot yet be established.

What it means

The honest formulation is the one the scientists use. Warming does not cause a specific glacier to fail on a specific Wednesday; it drives the heatwaves, retreat and permafrost loss that make such failures more likely, and it does so in terrain where nobody has built a warning system that operates on the timescale of a collapse.

3583 million children now face 20 extra heat-stress days a year

What happened

A study published 26 August in Science Advances, led by Rosa Pietroiusti of Vrije Universiteit Brussel, finds that 583 million children under ten — 44% of that age group worldwide — now experience at least 20 additional heat-stress days per year because of climate change, along with 190 million people aged 60 to 69, or 30% of that cohort. In today's climate 11% of under-tens face more than 100 extra heat-stress days a year; that rises to 13% at 1.5°C of warming and 23% at 2°C. The method uses wet-bulb globe temperature against a 28°C threshold, comparing the present climate with pre-industrial simulations.

Context

Exposure concentrates in South Asia, Southeast Asia and West Africa — the regions with the youngest populations, the least air conditioning and the smallest historical emissions. Pietroiusti notes the children affected “have contributed the least to historical greenhouse gas emissions.”

What it means

The jump from 13% at 1.5°C to 23% at 2°C is the number that should be read twice. Half a degree of global mean warming nearly doubles the share of small children living with more than a hundred extra dangerous-heat days a year. That is what the difference between the two Paris thresholds actually purchases.

Watch next

Whether the finding feeds into adaptation financing discussions in the COP process. The paper is at DOI 10.1126/sciadv.aeb3232; figures here are as reported by Carbon Brief, since science.org was unreachable.

4A heat wave over 80 million Americans, and what attribution can and cannot say about it

What happened

Extreme heat covered more than 80 million people across the south-central US and Southwest in the week to 25 August, with temperatures above 100°F in the Southern Plains and Lower Mississippi Valley, potentially above 110°F in the desert Southwest, and heat-index values of 105 to 115°F across parts of Arizona, Texas, Louisiana and northern Florida. Michael Wehner, senior scientist at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory: “For garden variety heat waves — like the hottest day of the year, or the hottest day every 10 years — in the U.S., climate change has increased that heat wave's temperature by 3 to 5 degrees Fahrenheit.” The average number of US heat waves has doubled since the 1980s.

Context

Wehner's framing is the useful one because it is about ordinary heat, not records. A three-to-five degree shift in the everyday distribution moves far more people across dangerous thresholds than any single record does.

5Gas storage at 62% against a 90% legal target, and the deadline is a window, not a date

What happened

EU gas storage stood at 62% full as of 20 August, against roughly 74% on the same date in 2025. Two corrections to how this is usually reported: the Gas Coordination Group's working benchmark is around 80% at the end of summer, with ten percentage points of flexibility either way under the revised regulation; and the binding obligation is 90% at some point between 1 October and 1 December, not a fixed 1 November trigger. Context

What it means

The flexibility built into the revised regulation is doing real work here: a hard 90% by 1 November would already be a crisis; a window to 1 December is a policy choice to avoid forcing member states to buy into a spike. The savings-rate fall alongside the consumption rebound is the diagnostic detail: French households did not get richer this quarter, they spent reserves. Profile

Kevin Warsh: the man who resigned over quantitative easing, having voted for it

On Thursday, in his hundredth day as chairman of the Federal Reserve, Kevin Warsh stood up at Jackson Hole and told a room of central bankers that the responsibility for 65 months of elevated inflation sits squarely with the institution he now runs. “And that is where it belongs,” he added. It was a striking thing to say about a period during which he was not there — and a slightly less comfortable one given that during the period he was there, he made almost exactly the same argument and then voted the other way.

He was born in Albany, New York in April 1970, took an AB from Stanford in 1992 and a JD from Harvard Law in 1995, and spent the next seven years at Morgan Stanley in mergers and acquisitions, leaving in 2002 as a vice president and executive director. In 2002 he married Jane Lauder — granddaughter of Estée Lauder, daughter of Ronald Lauder, and an executive who went on to run Clinique in her own right until 2024 — and joined the White House as special assistant to the president for economic policy and executive secretary of the National Economic Council. He is not an economist and holds no doctorate in the field. He is the second consecutive Fed chair of whom that is true.

In February 2006, at 35, George W. Bush put him on the Board of Governors; Dick Cheney administered the oath. He was widely reported at the time as the youngest person ever confirmed to the Board, a claim the Fed itself has never made and cannot settle, since it publishes no ages. Two years later he was one of the most important people in American finance. Ben Bernanke used him as the Board's emissary to Wall Street — Warsh's own later description, to Yale's financial-crisis oral history project, was “a key emissary to market participants, foreign central banks, and US political leaders.” He was the Fed's representative to the G-20 and its administrative governor. Bernanke, on his departure, cited his “intimate knowledge of financial markets and institutions” as “invaluable during the recent crisis.”

The unflattering account of the same years is on Senate letterhead. In April, ahead of his confirmation hearing, Elizabeth Warren wrote to demand Fed records and set out what she said they would show: that in August 2007 Warsh told colleagues “we don't see any immediate systemic risk issues that are brought to bear”; that he helped arrange JPMorgan's takeover of Bear Stearns; that he facilitated Goldman Sachs's and Morgan Stanley's emergency conversions to bank holding companies, having obtained an ethics waiver to deal directly with his former employer; and that during the FOMC meeting of 16 September 2008 he emailed a Treasury contractor and former Goldman banker about AIG's liquidity needs, after which AIG received an $85bn Fed loan in the amount that contractor had recommended. These are Warren's characterisations. They are also the most concrete account anyone has published of what he actually did.

Then the part everyone gets wrong. In November 2010 Warsh gave a speech to the securities industry called “Rejecting the Requiem,” warning that “as the Fed's balance sheet expands, it becomes more of a price maker than a price taker in the Treasury market,” and that he was “less optimistic than some that additional asset purchases will have significant, durable benefits for the real economy.” That same month, 23 conservative economists and investors published an open letter to Bernanke making the same case. Warsh did not sign it — he was a sitting governor — though the letter lives on the website of the Hoover Institution, where he later became a fellow, which is probably how the misattribution propagates. He resigned in February 2011 and the resignation was read as a protest against QE2. But he had voted for the January 2011 action, along with everybody else, and he was not among the voters in March. He never cast a dissenting vote against quantitative easing. He gave a speech and then he left.

That history is the key to what he is doing now, because he has come back determined to build the discipline he did not exercise. Since May he has abandoned forward guidance outright, cut the FOMC statement to about 130 words from the 300 or 400 it ran under Powell — “a bit shorter, a bit simpler” — and personally abstained from the Summary of Economic Projections, so that one fewer dot appears on the chart he says sends counterproductive signals. In July he announced five outside task forces, on communications, the balance sheet, data, productivity and jobs, and inflation frameworks, with recommendations due by year-end. The names include Mervyn King, Raghuram Rajan, Greg Mankiw, Thomas Sargent, Raj Chetty — and, on productivity and jobs, the venture capitalist Marc Andreessen and Walmart's Doug McMillon. A venture capitalist now sits on a Federal Reserve policy task force.

What he has not done is move interest rates. Two meetings, two holds at 3½ to 3¾ percent — unanimous in June, then 9–3 in July with Beth Hammack, Neel Kashkari and Lorie Logan dissenting in favour of a rise. For a chairman who talks this much about prices, a hundred days of no action is an awkward record, and his critics note that at his confirmation hearing he dismissed the Fed's preferred PCE gauge as a “rough swag,” then leaned in July on a trimmed-mean measure reading 2.2% while headline PCE stood at 3.7%. The charge is that he is shopping for the number he likes. His defence, offered at Jackson Hole, was a sentence worth keeping: “I stand here today committed to a discipline, not to a decision.” He also warned, about the market-based inflation expectations everyone is relying on, that they “tend to look strong and durable until they don't.”

The detail to read aloud: the Warsh family investment vehicle, TAW Ventures, is a pet-wellness fund. Its own website explains the initials — “TAW Ventures is named after my beloved Goldendoodle — Thaddeus Alistair Warsh.” Last October it put £2.5m into a British fresh dog-food company. The chairman of the Federal Reserve has a dog with his surname and a fund named after the dog. The FOMC next meets on 15 and 16 September, a meeting that carries a Summary of Economic Projections — the one he will not be contributing to.
