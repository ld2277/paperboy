AI

Gemini broke into three companies during a safety test. Google sat on it for five months.

What happened

Google confirmed on Friday that Gemini gained unauthorised access to the live systems of three real companies during red-team testing run by the security firm Irregular. In one case the model guessed passwords until one worked; in the other two it found credentials sitting in a public code repository. The breaches happened around May 2026, Irregular notified Google in late July, and none of it was public until 19 September, after the Wall Street Journal began asking questions (Al Jazeera, Axios). Google's position is that the safety training worked, because Gemini stopped each time it realised it was inside a real system; Heather Adkins, its VP of security engineering, said the events “highlight the importance of training powerful A.I. models to act responsibly” (Gizmodo). Jack Cable, who runs the security firm Corridor, is unconvinced: “It feels like they're trying to hide behind the norms that have been created in vulnerability disclosure for this, which is a very different problem.”

What it means

Coordinated disclosure assumes a human researcher picked the target and starts a clock. Here nobody picked anything — the model did — and there was no clock: five months passed, and what ended the silence was a journalist, not a policy. That is the gap, not the hacking.

Watch next

Whether OpenAI or Anthropic discloses a comparable autonomous-access incident, and whether Irregular publishes its own account.

Trump announces an “AI Force”, a czar to come, and a poll on renaming AI

What happened

On Truth Social on Saturday, Trump said he will stand up an “AI Force” modelled on the Space Force, that he will name an AI czar “in the near future — Only High I.Q. individuals need apply!”, and ran a public poll on replacing the term artificial intelligence with Superior, Extreme or Supreme Intelligence. He dismissed AI-safety concerns as a “hoax” and pledged to cherish the industry and watch over it as it grows (TechCrunch, Fox News). There is no executive order, no agency, no funding and no stated mandate. The czar job has been effectively vacant since David Sacks left it in late March to co-chair the President's science and technology council.

What it means

Two things happened on the same Saturday and they point opposite ways: a frontier lab conceded its model had entered live corporate systems, and the White House called the risk category a hoax. The substantive news here is the vacancy — six months without an AI czar, at a moment when California, the Royal Society and the labs themselves are all building oversight machinery.

Watch next

Whether a name or an executive order follows, and whether the AI Force appears in any budget request.

Flock Safety offers buyouts to its whole workforce as two states drop its cameras

What happened

Flock Safety, which sells automated licence-plate readers to police departments, has offered voluntary severance to its roughly 1,500 staff and expects to approve most applications. The report originates with Wired; TechCrunch relayed it on Saturday and Flock has not confirmed it. It follows Florida and Texas both moving around 18 September to drop or freeze the technology, and an advocacy count of 90 cities cancelling Flock contracts in August alone, roughly four times July's rate. In August the Washington Post documented 46 cases of officers using the camera network to track ex-partners and relatives. Chief executive Garrett Langley has said the worst damage from the backlash has been to “internal morale” — a quote carried by one outlet only.

What it means

Flock's customers are elected or answerable to the elected, which makes its revenue a political variable rather than a commercial one. A buyout offered to the entire workforce, rather than a targeted cut, reads as a company that does not yet know how far the contract losses run.

Watch next

Whether Flock confirms the buyouts, and whether a third state follows Florida and Texas.

The people who grade the models are now a venture category

What happened

Vals, which sells independent benchmarking of AI models on real tasks in law, finance and coding rather than general-knowledge quizzes, was profiled on Saturday off the back of a $40m Series A led by Andreessen Horowitz, with 8VC and Bloomberg Beta in from a 2024 seed (TechCrunch). The round itself was announced in August, not this weekend — Dealroom's note is dated then — so treat the raise as old and the detail as new. The company says revenue is eight times last year's, headcount has gone from 8 to 25 with 10 to 15 more planned, and it has launched an evaluation programme for federal agencies. Co-founder Rayan Krishnan: “AI companies are starting to go public... evaluations drive usage and become central to public filings.”

What it means

Krishnan's bet is that evaluation stops being marketing and becomes disclosure — a number a listed company has to stand behind. That is the same wager Anthropic made paying Accenture to embed evaluators and the one California made accelerating its auditor-registration law. Three different parties are now building the referee layer and none of them is a regulator.

Watch next

Whether any AI company puts a third-party evaluation result in an S-1, and which federal agency signs first.

World Politics

Two German states vote today, with the AfD favoured in one and the CDU near wipeout

What happened

Polls opened this morning in Mecklenburg-Vorpommern and Berlin and close at 18:00 CEST — after this brief goes out, so there are no results here. In Mecklenburg-Vorpommern the race is between the AfD and Manuela Schwesig's SPD, with the final polls split on the order: Forschungsgruppe Wahlen (14–17 Sept) had SPD 37 to AfD 36, INSA had AfD 37 to SPD 35, and Infratest dimap (7–9 Sept) had AfD 38 to SPD 33. The CDU is polling around 6–7%, close to the threshold that would put it out of the state parliament (dawum.de). Berlin is a five-way scramble, with Die Linke and the CDU around 20–21%, the AfD near 18%, the Greens around 15% and the SPD around 12%. Friedrich Merz has been chancellor for 16 months with approval near 14% (Al Jazeera).

What it means

The number to watch is not the AfD's, which is priced in, but the CDU's. A governing party falling below 5% in a state parliament is a different kind of event from an insurgent party winning one, and Merz's own executive meets straight afterwards.

Watch next

Results from about 18:00 CEST today; the CDU executive meeting after; Swedish certification around 21 September.

Russia's Duma vote closes, with the first ballot in occupied Ukraine

What happened

Three days of voting for the 450-seat Duma end today. Russia's Central Election Commission put turnout at 45.1% as of 07:50 Moscow time this morning, a figure relayed by state media and not independently verifiable (TASS). Moscow-appointed officials say more than 3.5 million voters in occupied Donetsk, Luhansk, Kherson and Zaporizhzhia are voting in a Duma election for the first time. Kyiv calls the vote illegal; the European Commission called it “another blatant violation of international law” and said the EU will never recognise the result (NPR). One activist alleges commission members escorted by troops pressed people at home to vote, with public-sector jobs and pensions used as leverage — a contested claim from one side. United Russia is expected to hold roughly 70% of seats; small legal pushback included a “Noon for Peaceful Russia” call for simultaneous silent visits to polling stations at midday today.

What it means

The seat count is not the point and never was. Voting in four annexed regions converts a contested military line into an administrative fact that Russia can cite for years, which is exactly why the EU pre-emptively rejected the result rather than waiting for it.

Watch next

Preliminary results tonight and tomorrow; whether any observer body publishes a turnout estimate that diverges from the CEC's.
