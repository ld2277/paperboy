AI

Trump is expected to hand the AI brief to his spy chief

What happened

Trump is expected to name Jay Clayton — the sitting Director of National Intelligence, former SEC chairman and former US Attorney for the Southern District of New York — as his AI czar, Axios reported on Friday, with an announcement possible within days. The job has been vacant since venture capitalist David Sacks resigned earlier this year. Trump, on the role: “Only High I.Q. individuals need apply!” A White House official pushed back on the reporting — “Any personnel announcement will be announced directly by the President. Any reporting until then is baseless speculation” — and as of Saturday morning no formal announcement had issued. Axios notes it is unclear whether Clayton would keep the DNI role or hold the AI post as a second title.

What it means

If it happens, the same person runs the intelligence community and AI industry policy. That is a structural answer to a question Washington has been circling all year: is frontier AI an economic file or a national-security one. Clayton said on 30 September that “super intelligence is a national security issue,” which tells you which way he would take it. An SEC alumnus also reads the disclosure and liability questions differently than a venture capitalist did.

Apple restricts macOS disk access, citing AI agents by name

What happened

Apple said on Friday it will tighten the macOS “Full Disk Access” permission, requiring “very explicit user action” before an app can reach files, messages, mail and browsing history. It named the reason: “Some developers are using Full Disk Access in ways that could put users at risk, exposing everything on their systems…without users’ full knowledge and understanding.” And: “As AI agents become increasingly capable and autonomous, the risks associated with this level of access will grow substantially.” Apple did not say which macOS release carries the change. The announcement followed a report by journalist Jason Aten that Meta’s Muse app read his private messages without explicit permission — a claim Meta disputes.

What it means

The first major OS vendor to restrict system access specifically on agentic-AI grounds. Desktop agents sell on exactly the capability Apple is now gating, so the product category gets a permission wall between it and the user’s data. Expect the same logic at Microsoft.

Two per cent of American households pay for AI

What happened

Only 2% of US households pay for an AI product, against 41% of US workers who use one, per figures circulated Friday. The gap is between use and payment: adoption at work is running at scale while household willingness to pay has barely moved. The datapoint landed in the same week the administration rebranded the field “super intelligence” by executive order — an order that, in the week’s most on-the-nose detail, set off a run on Slovenia’s .si domain as founders grabbed “super intelligence” names.

What it means

Consumer AI is being funded as though it were a subscription business and consumed as though it were a utility someone else pays for. The money is in enterprise seats, and the capex being raised against consumer demand is the part of the stack with the thinnest revenue underneath it.

Semiconductors

Samsung is reportedly asking roughly three times HBM3E pricing for HBM4

What happened

Korea’s Maeil Business reported that Samsung is seeking roughly three times its HBM3E price for HBM4, with volume commitments for 2027 already settled through long-term annual contracts and price negotiation happening this month. The quoted figures work out to between 2.7 and 3.3 times, so treat “three times” as the ceiling rather than the floor; the English pickups also disagree on whether the unit is per gigabyte or per gigabit, which is a large difference. No customers are named, Samsung has said nothing, and everything traces back to the one Korean report. Unconfirmed.

What it means

It points the same way as TrendForce’s 2027 HBM ASP revision and Micron’s pre-sold 2027 bits: memory makers are selling next year’s supply at next year’s prices, and the buyers are signing. For anyone pricing accelerators, HBM is becoming the bill of materials line that sets the floor.

Amazon is reported to be moving $8bn of Nvidia chips off its own balance sheet

What happened

Amazon is putting roughly $8bn of Nvidia Grace Blackwell chips into a special-purpose vehicle and leasing them back, according to a Financial Times report carried on Friday. The SPV would raise debt from outside investors, with Amazon taking an equity stake of up to 10%. The chips are already installed across more than a dozen US data centres in five states, Nevada and Virginia among them. No investors are named. It follows Anthropic’s arrangement a day earlier with Broadcom, which agreed to lend up to $42bn against a five-year chip leasing commitment. FT-sourced and not independently confirmed.

What it means

The hyperscalers are turning installed accelerators into a financeable asset class and taking the depreciation off their own books. That requires lenders to underwrite a residual value for used GPUs — which is a market that barely has public price discovery. If this structure spreads, somebody has to start publishing what a two-year-old Blackwell is worth.

Business

September payrolls came in at 29,000, and July turned into a job loss

What happened

US nonfarm payrolls rose just 29,000 in September against a consensus near 84,000, and the unemployment rate ticked up to 4.2% from 4.1%, the Bureau of Labor Statistics reported on Friday. The revisions did more damage than the headline: July was cut by 31,000, from +21,000 to minus 10,000 — an outright job loss — and August by 29,000, to +133,000. Average hourly earnings rose five cents, or 0.1%, to $37.81, up 3.0% over the year. Participation was 61.8%; U-6 fell to 7.6%. Gains were narrow: health care +17,000, construction +11,000, manufacturing +9,000, financial activities minus 7,000.

What it means

Three sectors supplied more than the entire headline. With wages at 3.0% there is no wage-push inflation left to point at, which matters because this Fed has been raising rates — the range is 3.75–4.00% after a 12-0 hike in September. The labour market is not cracking, but it has stopped generating jobs, and the committee is now tightening into a flat print.

Stocks rose on the weak print. So did yields

What happened

All three indices closed higher on Friday: the S&P 500 at 7,722.72, up 0.73%; the Dow at 51,176.96, up 0.49%; the Nasdaq Composite at 27,190.86, up 1.19%. Yields went the other way. Treasury’s own par yield curve has the 10-year at 5.28%, up four basis points from 5.24%, the 30-year at 5.63% and the two-year up five basis points to 4.83%. Yields fell on the jobs number and then reversed. November WTI settled at $91.26, down 1.73%, giving back part of Thursday’s spike; December Brent settled at $102.25, essentially flat.

What it means

In a market priced for hikes, a bad jobs number is good news for equities — that part is coherent. The curve shifting up anyway is not, and it is the more interesting signal: if weak data cannot pull the long end down, what is holding it up is term premium and fiscal supply rather than any view on growth. Oil above $90 keeps the inflation input live.

Nike’s China revenue fell 22%, and it named a restructuring plan

What happened

Nike’s first quarter of fiscal 2027, reported after Wednesday’s close and traded on Friday, put revenue at $11.213bn, down 4% as reported and 5% currency-neutral. Greater China fell 22% to $1.180bn, down 26% currency-neutral, while North America rose 2% to $5.127bn. GAAP diluted EPS was $0.48 against $0.49; net income $712m, down 2%; gross margin widened 60 basis points to 42.8% on lower warehousing and logistics costs. Alongside it Nike introduced “Pace,” targeting about $2.5bn of cumulative savings through fiscal 2031 against roughly $1.0bn of pre-tax charges over the same period, with about $0.3bn of those charges in fiscal 2027. No headcount was disclosed. The 8-K is here. The stock fell 3.64% to $33.87 on a day the market rose.

What it means

A 22% drop in a market Nike spent a decade building is not a cycle, it is share loss to domestic brands. “Pace” is the tell: $1bn of charges to save $2.5bn over five years is a company budgeting for a smaller cost base, not a recovery. Watch the gross margin line — it improved on logistics, not on price.

Startups

PaleBlueDot AI raises $200m at $3.2bn on $5bn of claimed contracts

What happened

The Palo Alto compute company closed a $200m Series C at a $3.2bn valuation, led by ComputeCore with existing shareholder B Capital participating, taking it to roughly $360m raised across three rounds — the round is now in several outlets, off a 1 October company announcement. The number doing the work is the company’s own: more than $5bn in signed customer contracts as of 30 September. That is a demand figure, not recognised revenue, and nobody outside the company has verified it. Its Series B was $97.1m in January.

What it means

A 16x step from $97m to $200m in nine months, priced off a contract backlog rather than revenue. That is how GPU-compute companies are being underwritten right now: the asset is the signed commitment, and the valuation is a bet that the commitment converts and the hardware behind it holds its value.

A five-person lab is selling crash-test dummies for chatbots

What happened

Circuit Breaker Labs launched on Friday, run by siblings Shirali Nigam as CEO and Arul Nigam as CTO, with five people and no disclosed funding. It builds simulated-user agents that run “tens of thousand to hundreds of thousands of simulated interactions per day” against mental-health and other high-risk AI products. Arul Nigam described the failure they are hunting: “Those sorts of safety vulnerabilities, where people aren’t necessarily actively trying to break the system — they’re engaging in a natural way — and the system has context pollution or it doesn’t understand the nuance, and then takes really dangerous action.”

What it means

Not adversarial red-teaming but ordinary-use failure testing, which is the harder and more commercially relevant problem once liability attaches. If the Hawley–Murphy liability bill lands, this becomes a compliance purchase rather than a safety one — the same path product-safety testing took.

US

The Supreme Court opens Monday on whether states can sue oil companies for the climate

What happened

The term’s first argument, Monday 5 October at 10am Eastern, is the Boulder climate-liability case against ExxonMobil and Suncor, testing whether states and localities can sue oil companies for climate damages. Justice Alito has recused without explanation — he has previously stepped back from cases involving oil company holdings — leaving eight justices and the possibility of a 4-4 split that resolves nothing. Senator Sheldon Whitehouse put the stakes plainly: “If you shut down climate legislation and you shut down climate regulation, what’s left is climate litigation.” Columbia’s Michael Gerrard: “Every time the party control of the White House changes, the federal government does a 180 on climate policy.”

What it means

This is the term’s biggest commercial case and it is first on the calendar. Dozens of suits in state courts are stayed behind it. An eight-justice bench makes the outcome harder to read, and a tie leaves the circuit split standing — the worst result for anyone trying to price the liability.

Four courts ruled against the administration in a day. The Senate recorded nothing

What happened

A federal judge reinstated Roger Rogoff as US Attorney in Seattle, finding his firing unlawful; a DC judge barred any future prosecution of David Hearn over the Lincoln Memorial Reflecting Pool vandalism; a judge dismissed a noncitizen-voting prosecution, holding the criminalising statute unconstitutional; and in the Broadview Six case a judge ordered further investigation into alleged White House contact with prosecutors — all four compiled by Just Security, each a separate docket, none independently confirmed here. Meanwhile the Senate recorded no roll-call vote on 1, 2 or 3 October; the last remains vote 256, the Sonderling confirmation on 30 September.

What it means

The pattern is consistent: executive personnel and prosecution decisions keep losing on process. And with the chamber producing no floor record at all, the courts are the only branch actually ruling on any of it. Funding runs out on 11 December with appropriations untouched.

The DNC sued the Pentagon over the overseas voter form

What happened

The Democratic National Committee sued the Defense Department over alterations to the federal post card application used by military and overseas voters, per Friday’s compilation — single-sourced here, and the complaint itself was not reached. It is a ballot-access fight with 32 days to the midterms, over a population that votes by a process with no in-person fallback. Separately, Florida’s attorney general sued Pfizer and chief executive Albert Bourla over Covid-19 vaccine safety claims.

What it means

Overseas and military ballots are small in number and decisive in close races, and they are the hardest to cure after the fact: a rejected form cannot be fixed from abroad in time. Expect this to be litigated on the clock rather than resolved.

World Politics

Israel’s Supreme Court put the Arab parties back on the ballot

What happened

The Court unanimously overturned the Central Elections Committee’s disqualification of Ra’am and the Joint List — Hadash, Ta’al and Balad — and reinstated Hadash MK Ofer Cassif by seven votes to two. Balad leader Sami Abu Shehadeh withdrew from the race himself after Court President Isaac Amit told him a majority would vote to ban him; the motions came from Likud and Itamar Ben-Gvir’s Otzma Yehudit. Abu Shehadeh afterwards: “What happened yesterday was a historical catastrophe for democracy, or the very narrow margins of democracy in the State of Israel.” Palestinian citizens are roughly a fifth of Israel’s population. The election is 27 October.

What it means

The ruling restores ballot access for the bloc representing 20% of the electorate three and a half weeks out, which matters arithmetically: Netanyahu’s bloc is leading in Friday’s Times of Israel poll with Likud tied for first, and the opposition cannot assemble 61 seats without these parties seated.

Brazil votes Sunday, and two Western governments shut their doors first

What happened

The United States suspended consular services in Brazil and Australia closed its embassy in Brasília, both effective Friday, citing security concerns before Sunday 4 October’s first round, with a runoff on 25 October. Brazilian federal police ran preventive operations in São Paulo and the Federal District over reported plans against US facilities but “found no evidence corroborating the initially reported threat, nor identified any suspects.” Workers’ Party president Edinho Silva called it interference: “It makes no sense at all. It’s one of Trump’s ways of trying to influence Brazilian public opinion.” Trump has said he is “watching that election very closely.” Lula faces Senator Flavio Bolsonaro, whose father is serving 27 years for attempting a coup.

What it means

The threat the closures cite is one Brazil’s own police could not substantiate, which makes the timing the story. Whatever the intent, closing the embassy 48 hours before a vote where the US has an obvious preference reads as a signal, and Brasília is reading it that way.

Russia hit the same Kyiv bridge for a third straight day

What happened

Russia struck a key Kyiv bridge on Saturday for the third consecutive day; Mayor Vitali Klitschko said “the bridge’s surface as well as trolleybus overhead power lines have been damaged,” with traffic halted — per the Kyiv Independent. Drones hit warehouses at Hostomel in Kyiv Oblast, reportedly pharmaceutical, injuring four including a child. On Friday glide bombs struck Kharkiv, killing women and injuring children, with Mayor Ihor Terekhov saying one bomb “completely destroyed” a residential building. Ukraine’s foreign ministry confirmed no diplomatic mission has requested evacuation. All casualty and damage figures are Ukrainian municipal accounts; no Russian account was issued.

What it means

Three days on one bridge is targeting, not spillover — the aim is the city’s internal movement rather than its power grid, which is a different and cheaper way to degrade a capital. Glide bombs on Kharkiv alongside it means Russia is spending guided munitions on urban targets at a tempo it previously reserved for the front.
