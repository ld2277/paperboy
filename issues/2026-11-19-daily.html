AI

Anthropic priced above its range at $83, and the filing itself makes it the biggest IPO on record

What happened

Anthropic priced its initial public offering at $83 a share on Wednesday, $5 above the top of the $70–78 range it had marketed, and filed the final prospectus at 06:05:50 ET this morning (424B4, accession 0001628280-26-074944). The deal was upsized to 980,000,000 Class A shares — 956,351,354 from the company, 23,648,646 from selling stockholders — against 825m in the preliminary document, with a 30-day option on 147,000,000 more. Gross proceeds of $81.34bn appear in the filing itself, rising to $93.54bn on full exercise. The listing is on Nasdaq, not the New York Stock Exchange, under “ANT”. Trading had not begun at our press time; the bell is 09:30 ET.

What it means

Al Jazeera calls it the largest initial public offering in history, ahead of SpaceX’s $75bn in June — though the same page puts SpaceX at $85.7bn once post-listing shares are counted, so the record holds base-to-base but not across the two. Pricing above the range is the real signal: the book was oversubscribed at the top. The $1.5 trillion valuation is fully diluted; on shares outstanding it is nearer $1.1 trillion, and which figure an outlet quotes tells you how it is counting.

Nvidia’s board has approached Arm’s chief executive about the vacancy

What happened

Bloomberg reported on Wednesday, citing a person familiar with the process, that Nvidia’s board has approached Arm chief executive Rene Haas about its vacant chief-executive role; we read it through MT Newswires. No response, refusal or candidacy is stated and no other candidate is named — one anonymous source behind one outlet. Freedom Capital Markets separately cut its price target to $230 from $305. EDGAR shows nothing new: a full-text search of Nvidia filings for 17–20 November returns only the single 17 November 8-K, with all 50 shards responding.

What it means

Going outside for the sitting chief executive of the company whose architecture Nvidia licenses would be a real departure, and would say the board wants an operator rather than a founder-figure. Treat it as a sounding, not a shortlist — and note the board still holds two positions it has never joined: its 8 November “target of a foreign intelligence effort”, and chair Harvey C. Jones’s account on the 17th of “disclosure of personal behavior inconsistent with the company’s code of conduct”.

Semiconductors

The remote-access chip rule published this morning and took effect the same day

What happened

Commerce’s interim final rule “Addressing Remote Access to Advanced Semiconductors” published in today’s Federal Register at 91 FR 74970 and is effective 19 November. Its three clocks now run from a real date rather than a bracket placeholder: comments due 18 January 2027; shipments already in transit under actual orders may complete by 19 December 2026; data centres already supplying covered companies must hold a licence by 17 February 2027. The 12,000,000 aggregate TPP threshold is in the rule text at §744.24(a)(1) and (e), measured at one site or across linked sites. The rule also formally rescinds the AI Diffusion Rule, revoking the model-weights controls, the country tiers, License Exception AIA and the Data Center VEU authorisation.

What it means

A rule released is not a rule in effect. This one is now both at once, which is what an interim final rule does and why it takes comments while already binding. The diffusion rescission is the larger half and got the least attention: the Biden-era framework that was never enforced is formally gone, replaced by a narrower control aimed at who rents the compute rather than who buys the chip.

Watch next

The 19 December in-transit cut-off, the 18 January comment deadline, and any named Chinese countermeasure.

The chip trade is broadening away from one name, which is healthier than it looks on a red day.

Business

Almost all of the Fed wants to raise again, and a couple would go more than a quarter point

What happened

The minutes of the 27–28 October meeting published on Wednesday. The committee held at 3.75–4.00% on a 10–2 vote, with Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack dissenting in favour of a quarter-point increase. “Almost all participants judged” that further action to raise the range “would likely be appropriate”; “a couple of participants” said an increase of more than 25 basis points could warrant consideration in December if inflation data were unfavourable; “a few” called October a close call. Staff had 12-month PCE inflation at 3.6% in September, core at 3.1%, and payroll gains averaging about 50,000 a month over three months. On the buildout: “Many participants viewed the AI buildout as a material source of inflation risk.”

What it means

This is a hiking cycle, and the minutes tighten it: December’s question is no longer whether but how much. An increase is about 93% priced, with 7% on a larger move — frenzycap’s futures read, timestamped midnight ET, an unvalidated compiler and above the roughly 4% tail we carried yesterday. Long yields nonetheless fell on a hawkish document, the 10-year to 5.07% and the 30-year to 5.41% with the 2-year unchanged at 4.80% (Treasury par curve).

Watch next

The 8–9 December meeting, which carries projections. The Fed’s own 2026 press-release index still shows no October minutes entry at all.

Wall Street split three ways, and oil fell 3% into a shooting war

What happened

Wednesday’s closes, from the AP index wire: the Dow 50,198.17, down 561.25 or 1.1%; the S&P 500 7,323.63, down 16.90 or 0.2%; the Nasdaq Composite 24,429.88, up 101.50 or 0.4%; the Russell 2000 2,714.31, up 12.44 or 0.5%. No outlet named a single largest drag. On Nvidia the same wire reads: “It initially jumped after delivering a better profit for the latest quarter than expected, but it quickly erased the gain and swung to a loss.” Oil fell anyway: December WTI settled at $83.26, down 3.2% and its lowest close since 26 August, and January Brent at $91.95, down 1.3% (Bloomberg energy wire), on a large US crude build.

What it means

The split is the information: a 1.1% fall in the Dow against gains in the Nasdaq and small caps is rotation, not a risk-off session. And the war premium that keeps not arriving is now a pattern worth naming — crude has fallen through a week in which Iran struck Kuwait, Bahrain and three tankers, which says traders are pricing the inventory build in front of them over the tail risk in the strait behind it. That is a bet, not a judgement, and it reprices in a single session.

Target and TJX both raised guidance, and most of Target’s raise is a tariff refund

What happened

Target had third-quarter net sales of $27,067m, up 7.1%, comparable sales up 5.6% on traffic up 5.0%, and GAAP and adjusted diluted EPS both at $2.65, against $1.51 and $1.78 a year earlier. It raised full-year EPS guidance to $10.65–$11.40 from $9.90–$10.90 — up at both ends — but the range now carries about $2.14 of tariff refunds against $1.65 before. Strip those out and the raise is 26 cents at the floor and one cent at the ceiling. TJX had net sales of $15,952m, up 6%, comps up 4% and GAAP EPS of $1.39; it lifted both full-year EPS floors by two cents, left the ceilings alone, raised its comps outlook to +4% from +3–4%, and reaffirmed an adjusted pretax margin of 12.0–12.1%. Walmart reports at 7 a.m. ET, after we publish.

What it means

Third big-box retailer running where the tariff refunds are doing the work — 34 cents of Lowe’s quarter, $2.14 of Target’s full-year range, 16 cents of TJX’s. These are one-off recoveries of duties already paid, and TJX’s fourth-quarter guide assumes no more of them, which is the honest way to show it. Underneath, Target’s traffic up 5.0% is the figure that is hard to fake.

Startups

Variational raised $110m at a $1.5bn valuation, with a single named lead

What happened

The on-chain markets firm Variational announced $110m at a $1.5bn valuation “led by Bain Capital Crypto, with support from Dragonfly, HSG, Peak XV, 50T Funds, and more”, datelined George Town, Cayman Islands (Business Wire). One lead, named, which is rarer than it should be. Two cautions: the release does not say whether $1.5bn is pre- or post-money; and its release ID begins 20261116 while the listing shows 18 November, so the round may be two days older than it looks.

What it means

A $1.5bn mark on $110m is roughly 7% sold — a founder-friendly round, and a sign the lead competed for it. The missing pre- or post-money label matters more than it sounds: on a raise this size the two readings are nearly $110m apart in implied pre-money, which is the difference between a good round and a great one.

Pulley raised $64m as two separate rounds on the same day

What happened

Pulley, which sells real-estate development software, announced $64m in one release that is actually two financings: “a $41 million Series B led by Jump Capital, with participation from Catalyst Investors, and a $23 million Series A led by CRV and Susa Ventures” (Business Wire, San Francisco, 18 November). So a $41m Series B with one lead and a $23m Series A jointly led by two. The release states no valuation, and we are not importing one.

What it means

Simultaneous A and B rounds usually mean two tranches of one negotiation, or two products financed separately — either way the headline $64m is not a single round and should not be compared with one. What a release withholds is reportable, and two more from the same day make the point: VitriVax took $87m “co-led by F-Prime, NEA, RA Capital Management, and Sanofi Ventures” (four co-leads, no lead, no valuation), and BrightAI’s $97m from Smith Point Capital is given no stage at all — not a Series B, not a C, just an “investment”. No lead usually means no one firm would take the price alone; no letter usually means the company would rather not place the round on the conventional ladder.

US

The US–Thailand trade agreement was not signed, and the published text carries no numbers

What happened

The White House published a joint statement on a US–Thailand reciprocal trade agreement, dated 17 November. It does not describe a signed agreement: the two governments “have reached substantial conclusion in their negotiations” and “will now begin their respective necessary internal processes to prepare the ART for signature and entry into force”. The published text contains none of the specifics — no US tariff rate on Thai goods, no share of US goods receiving Thai tariff elimination, no named agricultural products, no digital-trade provisions, no timeline. A “Thailand removes tariffs on 99% of US goods” figure is circulating; it is in no primary text we can reach.

What it means

We flagged this yesterday as the Asia trip’s one concrete deliverable. It is less than that. A joint statement recording substantial conclusion is a real step — it normally means the hard trade-offs are settled and lawyers are drafting — but it binds nobody and it can still slip. Treat any tariff number attached to this over the next few days as unsourced until a text appears.

Watch next

Publication of the agreement text, and any date set for signature.

The Senate spent Wednesday on one bill and got cloture, not passage

What happened

Three Senate roll calls on 18 November, all on H.R. 2347, which would exclude certain sexual-abuse damages from gross income: a motion to waive budgetary discipline agreed 70–26, the Capito substitute agreed 69–27, and cloture agreed 65–31 (roll-call list). Cloture is not passage, and no final vote had been held when our window closed; none are listed for today. On the 17th the Senate confirmed Anna St. John to the Fifth Circuit, 52–46. In the House, the Republican cloakroom’s sheet for Wednesday’s first vote series shows one vote — H.R. 9640, the Earth MRI Reauthorization Act, 396–14 (vote sheet). That sheet gives no roll-call number and does not state the question put, so we report the tally and not a passage.

What it means

Three roll calls to reach cloture on a narrow tax exclusion, with 65 votes for it, is a Senate treating floor time as the scarce resource it is — the bipartisan margins say the bill is not controversial, which makes the procedure the story. A correction to our own note: the 3 p.m. last-votes cut-off attaches to Friday, not Wednesday.

World Politics

Trump, Xi and Putin met in Shenzhen, and a “peace declaration” exists with one published sentence

What happened

The three presidents met at Kylin Villa, Shenzhen at 2:30 p.m. local time today, on the APEC sidelines and after a Trump–Xi bilateral at 12:45 p.m., exactly as the White House pool guidance had it. Xinhua confirms the meeting and mentions no joint document. TASS reports a “peace declaration” and publishes one sentence of it: the three “exchanged in-depth views on the current international situation and are committed to maintaining strategic stability and international security”. No government has published a text. Al Jazeera adds, citing unnamed Chinese state media, that the three committed to “stable energy flows” between their countries (liveblog, no per-item timestamps) — a phrase in neither TASS nor Xinhua, and if both are rendering one Chinese-language text they are one source, not two. Trump, in opening remarks: “We are choosing a different course.” Xi called the meeting “groundbreaking”.

What it means

A first three-way meeting of the US, China and Russia is a real event whatever came out of it, and the thinness of the paper is itself the finding: a declaration whose only published sentence is a boilerplate commitment to strategic stability is a photograph with a caption, not an agreement. Two corrections to our own notes — there was no Trump–Putin bilateral today, the schedule lists only the PRC bilateral and the trilateral and the Putin meeting was Xi’s on the 18th; and Ukraine came up at the Trump–Xi bilateral, where Trump said both countries want the war to end.

Iran hit Kuwait and Bahrain again, and Hormuz traffic is a third of normal

What happened

Iranian attacks on Kuwait and Bahrain on Wednesday drew condemnation from Saudi Arabia, Qatar and the GCC, whose secretary-general Jasem Mohamed Albudaiwi held Iran “responsible for this escalation and its repercussions on regional security”. CENTCOM posted on X that no US troops were hurt, and an unnamed US defence official told Stars and Stripes the strikes “did not impact U.S. interests”; a second unnamed official told The War Zone there were “zero impacts” on US facilities. Iran’s military claimed via Tasnim that its drones hit Patriot systems at Ali Al-Salem Air Base in Kuwait — no visual proof was offered, and neither Kuwait nor any US command has confirmed it. Per UKMTO figures reported by Al Jazeera, traffic through the strait is about 65% below pre-war levels, with 308 crossings in the seven days to 11 November. Bahrain’s Sitra refinery has been hit six times since February; output is back above 80% of its 415,000 barrels a day.

What it means

The shipping number is the one that matters: a third of normal traffic through Hormuz is a sustained disruption rather than an incident, and it is doing more economic damage than the strikes. Acting Navy Secretary Hung Cao — testifying at his own confirmation hearing on the 17th — put the cost of insuring a single transit at $64m against $54,000 before the war. That figure is disputed in the report carrying it, with a Windward analyst querying it and S&P Global having estimated about $21m in July, so treat it as testimony rather than a market price.

Whether a US naval command in Manama speaks on the record. The Saudi-led coalition says it intercepted a Houthi cruise missile aimed at southern Saudi Arabia.

Russia sent 98 drones and anti-ship missiles at Ukraine overnight, and hit Odesa’s grid

What happened

Ukraine’s air force, via Interfax-Ukraine at 09:15 local time, says Russia attacked from 18:00 on 18 November with 98 strike drones — Shahed, Gerbera and Parodiya-type decoys, 61 of the Shaheds jet-powered — plus Oniks anti-ship missiles launched from Crimea, number not stated. As of 08:00, 69 drones were downed or suppressed and hits were recorded in 18 locations. This is explicitly an interim count: the air force says drones were still in the air. Separately, Russian strikes on energy facilities in the Odesa region cut power in two districts, and President Zelensky said supply is most difficult in Kharkiv and Sumy.

What it means

The missiles are the new part, and the Interfax index headline says drones only — Oniks are supersonic anti-ship weapons being fired at land targets from Crimea, a harder intercept than a Shahed and a sign Russia is spending inventory it cannot easily replace. Eighteen locations hit from 98 drones is a worse ratio than the night before, and it arrives as the grid goes into winter. Witkoff and Kushner have meanwhile warned Moscow it risks losing its chance at a deal.

Watch next

A completed overnight count. The Berlin coalition meeting today, which Zelensky says Trump’s representatives attend.
