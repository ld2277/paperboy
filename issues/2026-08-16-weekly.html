Top story of the week

The blockade becomes the policy: Washington tightens its grip on Hormuz, and starts talking about owning it

On Friday afternoon, at a law-enforcement event in Garden City, New York, President Trump departed from his prepared remarks to announce a plan for a body of water 6,500 miles away. “After we finish defeating Iran, which is being very badly defeated,” he said, “pretty soon I'll be declaring the Hormuz Strait a territory of the United States.” He added that the US already has “the blockade” and that “no ships get through unless we want them to.” No declaration followed, no executive order, no legal mechanism. But the remark was not an aside. It was the loudest expression yet of something that has been happening quietly for a month: the American naval blockade of Iran has stopped being a wartime measure and become the entire policy.

The war began in late February. An interim agreement on the strait collapsed in mid-July, and the blockade of Iranian ports was reimposed. What that means in practice is visible from orbit. Loading at Kharg Island — which handles roughly nine-tenths of Iran's crude exports — halted on 31 July, all three berths empty on satellite imagery reviewed by the tanker trackers Kpler, Energy Aspects and Windward. It is warships, not paperwork, that emptied those berths. CENTCOM said on 12 August that it had redirected 55 merchant ships, disabled three and boarded two. Traffic through the strait itself has thinned to roughly eleven transits a day in the week of 3–9 August, down from 95 the week before, according to Lloyd's List Intelligence.

Prices have followed, though less violently than the rhetoric suggests. Brent traded around $84.11 a barrel on 10 August, ran up to nearly $89.60 midweek and settled at $88.52 on Friday, about a third above a year ago. That is serious for an American already paying nearly 25% more for petrol than in 2025, but it is not the $150 spike Hormuz scenarios have promised for two decades. The market has concluded the strait is not closed so much as rationed, and that the ships kept out are mostly Iranian.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent set out what comes next in a Newsmax interview aired on 13 August. The plan, he said, is “a combination of economic isolation like the world has never seen before, and the continued blockade in the Strait of Hormuz that will keep anything from going in or out of the Iranian ports,” with further announcements promised for the coming week. Trump, the same day, described himself as “low-keying it” and “semi-negotiating,” citing Iran's inflation. The administration is running a maximum-pressure campaign while insisting a deal is still available.

Tehran's answer was fast and unusually well-turned. Deputy Foreign Minister Kazem Gharibabadi wrote that the strait “cannot be taken over by a tweet, nor by an aircraft carrier, nor by issuing a decree, nor by an election speech,” adding that Iran “neither fears threats nor cowers before displays of power.” Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi — profiled below — had posted the day before that “worse than fake news is fake intelligence.” Iran's judiciary called the territorial claim “nonsense.” Araghchi has conditioned any reopening on sanctions relief and, at points, war reparations: the strait is now Iran's only remaining bargaining chip and it intends to charge for it.

“The Strait of Hormuz cannot be taken over by a tweet, nor by an aircraft carrier, nor by issuing a decree, nor by an election speech.” — Kazem Gharibabadi, Iran's deputy foreign minister

On the legal question there is not much argument. Bruce Fein, a former Reagan Justice Department official, notes that annexing territory requires “a treaty ratified by the Senate or statute”; the law-of-the-sea scholar Natalie Klein says the US would need to occupy the Iranian coastline for even a colourable claim, and that the attempt would breach the UN Charter. The strait's navigable channels run through Iranian and Omani waters, and the transit-passage regime governing them exists precisely so no coastal state can charge tolls or close the door. Declaring it American property would not extend that regime, it would demolish it — and the country with most to lose from a world where straits belong to whoever parks a carrier in them is the United States.

The costs land unevenly. Gulf exporters and Asian refiners absorb the freight and insurance premiums; Iran absorbs the export halt, with 26 of its flagged tankers reportedly idling off Malaysia; American drivers absorb the pump price. So does the Navy. The USS Abraham Lincoln has been deployed 265 days and at sea 241 consecutive days, breaking the service's previous record of 206, its last port call in Guam on 11 December. The Navy concedes that “traditional supply hubs in the Middle East were disrupted by combat actions” and that it triaged resupply “first food, then hygiene items, then mail.” The USS George Washington is transiting west and is expected to relieve it, though the Pentagon has not confirmed that.

The case for reading this week as an escalation is not the rhetoric — rhetoric is cheap and this president produces a great deal of it. It is that the blockade has outlived every objective it was supposed to serve. It was a lever to force a deal; there is no deal, and Araghchi says no decision has even been taken to resume talks. It was temporary; it is a month old in its current form and Bessent is promising to deepen it. A blockade with no exit is not a negotiating tactic, it is a siege, and sieges end either in capitulation or in something breaking. The Yemen file this week — six dead on the cargo ship Tihamah, the UN envoy warning of the worst risk of full-scale war since 2022 — is a preview of what breaking looks like in a waterway.

Three dates. Bessent's promised sanctions package, due this coming week, will show whether “economic isolation like the world has never seen” means secondary sanctions on Chinese buyers — which would turn an Iran policy into a China policy overnight. The FOMC meets 15–16 September with a rate hike still priced as live, and an oil-driven inflation impulse is exactly what would settle that argument for the hawks. And the midterms are on 3 November, with the president's approval on the economy at 32%, down from 40% at the start of the term. A war that raises petrol prices without producing a surrender is a difficult thing to run on.

The interesting question is not whether the partnership works but what margin the labs give away to get distribution — and IBM's insistence on staying model-agnostic suggests it intends to keep that leverage.

2Anthropic will watermark text from future Claude models, globally, to meet EU rules

What happened

Anthropic said on 14 August that it will embed invisible watermarks in text generated by its models and use the C2PA standard for files, applying the policy worldwide rather than scoping it to Europe — “because we don't yet have a durable way to scope it by region.” The mark applies to models launched from here on, with older models to be retrofitted “over the coming months,” and covers the API, the Claude apps, Claude Code and Claude Cowork. The company says the watermark travels with copied-and-pasted text and “may persist through some editing.” (Anthropic)

Context

The driver is the EU AI Act's transparency obligations, which took effect on 2 August 2026, and the accompanying Code of Practice on transparency of AI-generated content that Anthropic signed in July. Google, Meta, Microsoft, OpenAI, Black Forest Labs and Synthesia have made similar commitments.

Who's affected

Anyone who publishes text produced with Claude, including newsrooms, law firms and universities; detection-tool vendors, whose product just got a legitimate signal to work with; and Chinese and open-weight model providers, who are under no such obligation.

What it means

The durability question is unresolved and Anthropic has not quantified it — how much editing removes the mark is exactly the number that determines whether this is a compliance artefact or a real forensic tool. But note the shape: a European regulation has produced a global product change at an American company, because scoping by region was harder than complying everywhere. That is the Brussels effect working as designed.

3DeepSeek quadruples its API prices, ending the cheapest-inference era

What happened

DeepSeek raised API prices sharply effective 16 August, abandoning flat pricing for a peak/off-peak structure. Startups that built unit economics on sub-$0.30 inference; Chinese enterprise buyers, who now face a genuine peak-hour surcharge; and every Western lab that has been told for eighteen months that its pricing floor was set in Hangzhou.

What it means

The most plausible read is capacity, not greed: a company that has to ration by time of day is a company short of chips. Either way the thesis is dead. Inference is not free, it is not going to be free, and the compute constraint applies on both sides of the export-control line.

5OpenAI splits its cyber offering into Blue and Red tiers, with an exploit-capable model for a vetted few

The allowlist is the whole argument. It concedes that the capability is dangerous while betting that concentrating it in four large vendors is safer than either withholding it or releasing it openly. Climate Change

1Copernicus: July set a global ocean-heat record, and western Europe had its hottest June–July ever

What happened

Published 10 August. Sea-surface temperature across the extra-polar ocean averaged 20.96°C in July — the highest July on record, beating 20.89°C in 2023. Global surface air temperature was 16.90°C, 1.47°C above the 1850–1900 baseline, making it the joint second-warmest July on record. The standout: western Europe's combined June–July average of 21.62°C, 2.79°C above the 1991–2020 normal and a record for the region. Samantha Burgess, Strategic Lead for Climate at ECMWF: “July 2026 was the third consecutive month of exceptional heat in western Europe... As soils dry out, they lose their ability to provide natural cooling, allowing heat to build more readily.” (Copernicus)

Context

Note the two different baselines in that paragraph — 1.47°C is against pre-industrial, 2.79°C is against the modern 1991–2020 average. They are not comparable and are frequently conflated. Arctic sea ice was sixth lowest for July, Antarctic fifth lowest.

Who's affected

Mediterranean fisheries and coral systems; European agriculture, already facing cut crop forecasts; anyone on a coast where record sea-surface temperatures load the dice for storm intensity.

What it means

Burgess's point about soil moisture is the mechanism worth internalising. Dry soil stops evaporating, evaporation is what cools the surface, so drought amplifies heat which deepens drought. It is a feedback, not a coincidence, and it is why western Europe keeps producing records rather than merely hot summers.

Context

Romania is now leaning on Bulgarian imports and domestic wind. The plant's director said he did not expect a restart within ten days. Reporting rests largely on a single outlet chain because Nuclearelectrica's own disclosures were not reachable.

Who's affected

Romanian households and industry facing a fifth of baseload offline in a heatwave; Bulgaria's grid; the wider Balkan interconnection, which absorbs the imbalance.

What it means

This is the cleanest illustration of the week's real theme. Thermal generation — nuclear, coal, gas — needs water, and water is the thing climate change is taking away first. A country that decarbonised via nuclear finds its low-carbon baseload is also the part that fails in a drought. There is no version of the energy transition that does not have to solve for cooling.

2The EU packaging regulation bites — and the Commission misses its own labelling deadline on day one

What happened

The Packaging and Packaging Waste Regulation became directly applicable in all 27 member states on 12 August, replacing national regimes built on the 1994 directive. Two provisions bit immediately: caps on PFAS “forever chemicals” in food-contact packaging, and traceability duties allowing authorities to identify packaging producers from shipping documents. The same day, the Commission missed its own 12 August deadline to adopt the implementing acts designing the harmonised EU waste-sorting label; the draft is now expected in Q4 2026, pushing the labelling obligation past 2028. Environment Commissioner Jessika Roswall conceded that “new rules also come with adjustment costs.” (Brussels Signal)

Context

Forward deadlines: by 2030, single-use plastic bottles must contain at least 30% recycled content, beverage distributors must hit 10% reuse, all packaging must be recyclable and states must cut per-capita packaging waste 5%; by 2040, a 15% cut against 2018. Europeans currently generate about 180kg of packaging waste per person per year, 35.3kg of it plastic.

Who's affected

Every consumer-goods company selling into the EU; German retail body Händlerbund says the regime is “piling on obligations instead of removing them,” objecting to per-country registration with no single EU licence; recyclers, who gain a guaranteed feedstock market.

What it means

The missed deadline is the substantive story. A regulation whose consumer-facing element — the sorting label — slips past 2028 while its compliance burdens land immediately is a regulation that front-loads cost and back-loads benefit. That is the specific failure mode the Commission's own simplification agenda was supposed to address.

5Coal falls to a record-low 9.2% of EU electricity — with no dividend for bill-payers

What happened

Eurostat data reported on 14 August put combined coal at 9.2% of EU electricity, its lowest share since 1990: hard coal at 3.7% (105,601 GWh) and brown coal at 5.5% (154,186 GWh). Gas overtook coal in 2019 and now sits second behind nuclear; hydro and wind passed coal in 2023, solar in 2024. Households have seen none of it: average EU household electricity was €28.96 per 100 kWh in the second half of 2025, up from €28.79 in the first half and well above pre-2022 levels, with taxes and levies at 28.9% of the average bill. Context

The gap between falling emissions and rising bills is the central political vulnerability of European climate policy, and it is entirely explained by grid costs, taxes and the marginal-pricing mechanism rather than by the cost of renewables themselves.

Who's affected

European households and energy-intensive industry; Polish and German coal regions; the political parties campaigning against the Green Deal on cost grounds.

What it means

Nine per cent coal is a genuine achievement and almost nobody will feel it. Until the decarbonisation dividend shows up in a bill, the politics of the transition will keep getting harder even as the physics gets easier — and taxes and levies at 28.9% of the bill are a policy choice, not a law of nature.

Profile

Abbas Araghchi: the carpet-seller's son who has spent thirty years learning to say no politely

When the Iran nuclear deal was signed in Vienna in the summer of 2015, the cameras found Javad Zarif: foreign minister, fluent American-educated charmer, the face of the thing. Behind him, in the frame but not of it, stood a shorter, greyer man with a clipped beard who had done much of the actual negotiating and almost none of the talking. Eleven years later Abbas Araghchi has Zarif's job, no deal to defend, an American blockade around his country's ports, and a president in Washington announcing he intends to annex the Strait of Hormuz. He is the most consequential Iranian official most Westerners could not pick out of a line-up.

He was born in Tehran on 5 December 1962, into a wealthy bazaari family in the carpet trade with ties to the conservative Motalefeh, the Islamic Coalition Party. This matters more than it sounds. Iran's foreign-policy establishment is usually sorted into technocrats and revolutionaries; Araghchi is neither, exactly. He is bazaar — a merchant-class conservative from the milieu that bankrolled the 1979 revolution and then spent forty years quietly trading with everyone. People from that world are not romantic about ideology and not sentimental about deals. They are interested in terms.

He was sixteen when the revolution came and seventeen when Iraq invaded, and he served in the Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps through the eight-year war. Precisely what he did is unknown — his own ministry's official CV omits military service entirely, which is itself telling in a system where a war record is usually advertised. Afterwards he went into the foreign ministry's School of International Relations, took a master's in political science in Tehran in 1991, and then did something nobody predicts from that biography: he went to England. He completed a doctorate at the University of Kent in 1996, on the evolution of political participation in twentieth-century Islamic political thought, supervised by David McLellan — one of Britain's foremost scholars of Karl Marx. A former Revolutionary Guard wrote his thesis under a leading Marxologist in Canterbury. It is the most surprising fact about him, and it explains something in the way he argues: he is comfortable working inside someone else's framework, which is a rarer skill in diplomacy than it should be.

Then the apprenticeship abroad: ambassador in Helsinki from December 1999 to September 2003, concurrently accredited to Tallinn, then Tokyo from February 2008 to October 2011. Neither is glamorous, but both are places where a diplomat learns to work a room that is not shouting. He came home into the Rouhani era and in September 2013 was made deputy foreign minister for legal and international affairs — the technical seat at the nuclear table.

This is where he actually changed things. The JCPOA was not won at the podium; it was won in the annexes. Araghchi ran the working groups on the mechanics: enrichment levels, centrifuge counts, the sequencing of sanctions relief, the design of the snapback provision, the inspection protocols. He translated the Supreme Leader's red lines into text that could survive a room full of P5+1 lawyers. His own CV claims the title of chief nuclear negotiator from September 2013 to September 2021 — four years past the deal, which if true means he kept the file through the American withdrawal, the maximum-pressure campaign, the killing of Qassem Soleimani and the Vienna talks that tried to rebuild it. Others describe him more modestly as Zarif's deputy. Both agree on the substance: for the better part of a decade, when Iran said something technically precise to a Western government about its nuclear programme, Araghchi had written it.

There is an unflattering side to the story, and it should be said plainly. He serves a government that jails journalists, executes protesters and arms proxies whose attacks this week killed six people on a cargo ship in the Bab al-Mandeb. Making that government's positions sound reasonable to foreigners is what a foreign minister does, but it is not a morally neutral act. The IRGC service is a real part of the record even if the details are not public. The family has its own smudge too: his nephew Ahmad Araghchi, deputy governor of the central bank, was dismissed and arrested in 2018 during the currency crisis.

He became foreign minister on 21 August 2024, confirmed by parliament as President Masoud Pezeshkian's nominee, largely on the strength of being the one person left whom both hardliners and reformists could tolerate. Two years on, the job consists almost entirely of managing a war he did not choose and a blockade he cannot lift. In April he was in Islamabad for the ceasefire talks that produced the Islamabad Memorandum; it did not survive the summer. This week he was reduced to arguing definitions, telling Iranian media — in remarks carried by Mehr and worth treating with care given the translation chain — that “we did not have a ceasefire to extend” but rather “an end to the war, which has now entered a new situation,” and that no decision had been taken on resuming talks with Washington.

His most effective line of the week ran to nine words on X: “Worse than fake news is fake intelligence. Be careful.” It is a very Araghchi manoeuvre — no threat, no bombast, just a lawyer's reminder that the other side has been wrong before and is about to be wrong again, phrased so the American audience hears it in their own idiom. That is the method, honed over thirty years: never raise your voice, never concede a technical point, and let the person across the table work out for themselves that they are arguing with someone who has read the file more carefully. Whether the method has anything left to work with is the open question. A negotiator with nothing to trade is just a spokesman, and the strait is the last chip on his side of the table.
