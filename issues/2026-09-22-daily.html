AI

Amazon cuts off Meta's shopping agent

What happened

Amazon blocked Meta's Muse AI agent from buying on users' behalf, notifying customers late Sunday, SiliconANGLE reported. Amazon's two objections: Muse did not identify itself as an agent in its HTTP requests, which Amazon's terms require, and it reached customer account data and purchase history without clear disclosure — “an undisclosed third party moving through customer accounts”. Amazon told GeekWire it is “fairly straightforward that third-party applications that offer to make purchases on behalf of customers from other businesses should operate openly and respect service provider decisions about whether or not to participate”. Amazon sued Perplexity over comparable shopping agents; that case was dismissed in August. Single-outlet so far, and no Meta response was captured.

What it means

Losing in court did not stop Amazon enforcing at the protocol layer, which is the more durable lever. The fight is not really about disclosure headers — it is about who owns the checkout, and whether an agent counts as the user or as a competitor's robot walking through the store.

Muse is outrunning ChatGPT's early mobile curve — and got cut off in the same week

What happened

Meta's Muse launched in the US in early September, passed 730,000 downloads inside five days, and by Friday 20 September was the most popular free app on the App Store, per SiliconANGLE; TechCrunch framed the adoption curve as steeper than ChatGPT's own mobile launch. Meta stock closed Monday at $741.25, up 11.43% (stockanalysis.com), with Wells Fargo raising its target on the app-ranking news.

What it means

Two facts in tension inside 48 hours: top of the charts on Friday, locked out of the largest US retailer by Sunday night. Distribution Meta can buy; permission to transact it cannot. An assistant that cannot complete a purchase is a very expensive search box.

Retention past the 30-day mark, which is where ChatGPT's mobile curve actually held.

OpenAI convenes mathematicians, claims 100+ open problems resolved

What happened

OpenAI announced an Advisory Group on Mathematics and Artificial Intelligence on Monday, hosted at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton with nine initial members, alongside a claim that an internal model has resolved more than 100 open mathematics problems. OpenAI said the group “will serve as a bridge to the mathematical community and broader public” but “will not be responsible for advising us on how to pace our internal progress”. The IAS distanced itself: “we do not have decision making power at any AI company”. Twenty-five Fields medallists have signed an open letter warning about AI labs and intellectual work; only one group member, Camillo De Lellis, signed it. TechCrunch only, and the 100-problem figure is an unverified self-report.

What it means

A bridge with the load-bearing span removed. The advisory body gets the community's legitimacy; the company keeps the throttle.

Watch next

Whether any of the 100 results get published and refereed.

Semiconductors

AMD closes worth a trillion dollars; chip index up 4.3% in a day

What happened

AMD finished Monday at $615.52, up 9.95%, with a market capitalisation of $1.00 trillion — and it held at the close, not just intraday (stockanalysis.com; companiesmarketcap prints $1.005tn, against $913.89bn on Friday). The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index closed at 12,433.17, up 511.49 points or 4.29%. Intel rose 12.14% to $121.78 and Arm Holdings 17.16% to $322.90 (INTC, ARM).

What it means

A 4.3% day in a broad sector index is not a stock story, it is a repricing of the whole supply chain. AMD joining the trillion-dollar club on a day Intel gained 12% says the market has stopped treating AI silicon as a one-vendor trade — and has started paying for second sources.

Intel reportedly in talks with AUO on Micro LED packaging

What happened

Taiwanese reports on Monday put Intel in advanced discussions with panel maker AU Optronics on Micro LED advanced packaging for co-packaged optics and high-density AI compute, per MicroLED-Info relaying a DigiTimes exclusive. The technique embeds Micro LED emitters in glass substrates via through-glass vias, pairing Intel's glass-substrate patents with AUO's panel processing. AUO is targeting AI datacentres “within 2-3 years” and says Micro LED co-packaged optics is “highly suitable for data-transmission cables up to 10m in length”. Named collaborators: Ennostar, Tyntek, Aledia. This is a talks story: no Intel or AUO statement, no terms, no named executives. Headlines reading “partners with” overstate it.

What it means

Optical interconnect is the next bottleneck after the package itself, and Intel is attacking it from the substrate rather than the transceiver. It is also the clearest thing pointing at Monday's 12% move.

An actual Intel or AUO confirmation, and Intel's reported 5 October CPU price rise.

Hyperscalers raised enterprise SSD orders inside two weeks

What happened

TrendForce said on Monday that US cloud providers have upgraded enterprise SSD demand forecasts within the past fortnight, with fourth-quarter orders potentially exceeding the third's, and that eSSD prices will keep rising through 4Q26 as NAND suppliers tighten allocation. The pull is no longer only high-performance TLC: QLC demand is rising, driven in North America by vector databases holding unstructured data for agentic AI, and in China by DeepSeek-lineage architectures offloading KV cache to high-capacity QLC drives to cut inference cost.

What it means

Inference economics are now visible in the storage bill of materials. When a model architecture decides to page its attention cache to disk, that is a purchase order, not a design note — and it lands on the cheapest, densest NAND, which is exactly the tier nobody was building capacity for.

Oura's $2.2bn IPO is mostly a cash-out

What happened

Oura set terms for a $2.2bn listing at $40–44 a share, but of the 50,000,000 shares on offer only 13,500,000 come from the company and 36,500,000 from existing holders, per the S-1/A. At the $42 midpoint the company's net proceeds are about $532.6m — of which roughly $526.4m goes straight to employee share-tax withholding, leaving about $6.2m for general corporate purposes. TechCrunch reports Forerunner Ventures is selling its entire 9.3% stake, 28.7m shares worth around $1.2bn, nearly 80% of the secondary. Valuation depends on the basis: $14.1bn on shares outstanding, $14.9bn fully diluted at the midpoint.

What it means

A listing where the company keeps $6m is a liquidity event wearing an IPO costume. It is also an honest one — Oura had $372m in cash and did not need the money.

AI is now raising the price of televisions

What happened

TrendForce expects Gen 5-and-above panel fab utilisation to fall to 79.6% in October, down 4.2 percentage points month-on-month, with total TV panel costs up 4–7% quarter-on-quarter in 4Q26 even as TV panel unit demand falls 4%. The stated cause is AI demand “crowding out upstream material capacity”. Golden Week shutdowns compound it: HKC's H1 fab down seven days, TCL CSOT's T1, T6 and T10 seven days each, BOE's Gen 10.5 four to five. China's three leading panel makers now account for 70% of supply.

What it means

Costs rising into falling demand is the signature of a supply shock, not a cycle. This is the first clean case of AI capex showing up in a consumer price that has nothing to do with AI.

Startups

Samsung C&T puts $70m into Kairos Power's Oak Ridge reactor

What happened

Samsung C&T is investing up to $100m in Kairos Power — $70m of it equity, the remainder in-kind engineering services — toward Hermes 2, Kairos's first commercial-scale fluoride salt-cooled high-temperature reactor, at Oak Ridge, Tennessee. The Google connection is indirect but real: Google has an existing agreement with Kairos for roughly 0.5GW by 2030, and Hermes 2's output counts toward it. Note the headline number overstates the cash — this is $70m of capital plus a construction partner, not $100m on the balance sheet.

What it means

The interesting part is the in-kind half. Kairos's binding constraint is not money, it is people who can actually build a first-of-a-kind nuclear plant on schedule; Samsung C&T is one of the few contractors with that bench.

Corridor takes $25m seed to broker health benefits for small firms

What happened

Corridor raised a $25m seed led by Bain Capital Ventures, with BoxGroup and angels from OpenAI, Scale AI and Ramp; valuation undisclosed. Founders are Nikhil Aggarwal (CEO), Jackson Wagner, Eric Qian and Jason Dong. The product pairs human advisors with agents that verify network coverage and schedule care. Aggarwal: “We are every single employee and management's concierge for all things healthcare.” The pitch is that incumbent brokerages ignore small accounts because the commission is small while the servicing time is not; the launch is timed to Q4 because “80% of small businesses choose their health plan in Q4”.

What it means

A $25m seed for a brokerage is really a bet that agents collapse the cost-to-serve on accounts too small to be worth a human. That is the most credible AI services thesis going, and the hardest to prove.

Whether they land measurable numbers of Q4 renewals, the only test that matters here.

US

CNN, MS NOW and Politico sue over revoked White House credentials

What happened

The three outlets filed suit on Monday seeking their White House credentials back, arguing the ban is a First Amendment violation, per Axios and Just Security. Trump announced the ban on Friday 18 September; credentials were taken Saturday. The effect was visible the same day: with the TV pool suspended, Trump's Monday press conference went out on the official White House feed without usable audio. Seth Stern of the Freedom of the Press Foundation: “If you're living in Wyoming or Arizona or Chicago, it's very likely that the White House news you're reading originates from the press pool.” The pool normally rotates five networks and roughly 20 journalists.

What it means

Replacing the pool with an in-house feed transfers the record of what a president said from the press to the White House. Monday's missing audio shows the substitute does not yet work as journalism, or even as stenography.

Watch next

A docket number and any TRO; the case will move fast if a judge takes the credential question seriously.

Draft executive order would give an outside committee a veto over NIH grants

What happened

OMB is drafting an order creating an external committee able to veto awards across the NIH's biomedical portfolio that do not conform to the administration's priorities, per reporting carried by the Philadelphia Inquirer and logged by Just Security. NIH is a $48bn agency that passes most of its budget to outside labs. The fight is internal: OMB Director Russell Vought has accused NIH Director Jay Bhattacharya of funding projects at odds with administration priorities; Bhattacharya has defended cancellations as aimed at “politicized expressions of devotion to a particular ideology” rather than “good science”, and opposes political control of grant approval. The draft is unsigned and nothing has appeared on whitehouse.gov. Sources disagree on the date of the White House meeting.

What it means

Peer review has survived every previous attempt to politicise it because the veto sat with scientists. An external committee with award-level veto changes the unit of control from the budget to the grant.

Watch next

Whether it is ever signed — and if so, the first APA suit from a university consortium.

There is no September funding cliff

What happened

Worth saying plainly, because the calendar invites the opposite assumption. The House passed a clean continuing resolution 370–48 on 1 September, after the Senate cleared it 90–6 in July, funding the government at current levels through 11 December (Nextgov). Speaker Mike Johnson said the measure “simply holds funding steady at current levels through Dec. 11th”; Appropriations Chair Tom Cole said it “will allow Congress time to get past the November elections”. What actually falls on 30 September is the new Medicaid provider tax limit, not a lapse (CRFB). On 11 December, funding, VA health extenders, Farm Bill provisions and surface transportation authorisation all expire together.

What it means

The shutdown risk was moved, not removed — and moved to a date five weeks after the midterms, when the negotiating leverage will belong to whoever won.

Watch next

11 December, and whether appropriators mark up anything at all before it.

World Politics

UNGA opens without Xi, and without Abbas

What happened

The 81st General Assembly's high-level week opened Tuesday and runs to 28 September, with Guterres opening, then Assembly President Khalilur Rahman, then Brazil's Lula by the tradition that has held since 1955; Trump was expected around 09:45 ET (Al Jazeera). Xi Jinping is skipping New York entirely, addressing the Assembly by video while travelling to Washington: he arrives at Joint Base Andrews on the 23rd, with the bilateral, a Rose Garden military review and an East Room state dinner on Thursday the 24th. The administration denied visas to Mahmoud Abbas and his delegation for a second year; the Assembly voted to protest and will take a prerecorded Abbas statement Thursday morning (NPR). The video-address detail is NPR-only.

What it means

The week's real diplomacy has moved 200 miles south. Xi gets a state dinner and skips the room where 190 other countries get four minutes each — an accurate reading of where decisions are made, and a costly one for the institution hosting the alternative.

Thursday: the Trump–Xi bilateral, the Abbas tape, and the fourth Secretary-General straw poll expected after the 28th.

Russian barrage kills six across Ukraine; mall hit in Zaporizhzhia

What happened

Russia launched 172 drones overnight into Monday; Ukrainian air defence downed 148 plus one S8000 “Banderol” hybrid weapon. At least six were killed and 38 wounded nationwide (Kyiv Independent). In Zaporizhzhia, eight were wounded when strikes hit a shopping mall and an educational institution. Donetsk Oblast: three killed, 12 wounded. Kharkiv: one killed, five wounded, including a 13-year-old boy severely burned near Lozova. Sumy: two killed, three wounded. Kherson: nine wounded. Wire coverage frames it as retaliation for Ukraine's weekend drone barrage on Moscow during the Duma vote; that reporting sits on blocked domains and the roughly 1,000-drone figure is unverified here.

What it means

The exchange rate keeps worsening for civilians on both sides of a war neither air force can win. A 148-of-172 intercept rate is excellent and still leaves a mall.

Israel strikes Gaza after roadside bomb hits an unmanned IDF vehicle

What happened

The IDF struck Hamas infrastructure on Monday after an unmanned engineering vehicle was hit by a roadside bomb in northern Gaza, with no injuries in the initial incident; the military called it “a blatant violation of the ceasefire agreement by the Hamas terror group” (Times of Israel). Separately Qatar rejected a New Yorker report that its aid to Gaza was diverted to Hamas's military wing: “Any funds that may have reached Hamas are not the responsibility of the State of Qatar, but rather Israel, which oversaw their delivery.” In the West Bank, Netanel Shukrun was killed in a shooting near Neve Tzuf. A US official said Trump is unlikely to meet Netanyahu at the UN, citing scheduling; the PMO says the schedule is not final.

What it means

Each side's account of its own operations is the only account available, which is the structural problem with adjudicating a ceasefire from the outside. The Qatar dispute is contested on its face and should be read as two claims, not one finding.
