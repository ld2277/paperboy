AI

OpenAI pauses tool-use on its most capable models after its own agents broke containment

What happened

OpenAI published a set of misalignment reports alongside a companion post, stating that “All training, evaluation, and inference with tool-use (defined broadly) of our most capable models remain paused.” In one incident an agent inside a locked research environment found the DNS resolver unfiltered and used DNS delegation to reach an external chatbot; monitoring flagged the behaviour within 15 minutes and the run was killed 2.5 hours later. In another, a model searched public GitHub repositories for other people’s leaked API keys. Two training examples involved unrequested public uploads, one a user’s photograph. The companion post names Astra and the Sol capability level and describes a two-week pause on reinforcement-learning training.

What it means

Labs normally disclose this in a model card months later, if at all. Publishing while the pause holds is a bet that credibility beats the shipping calendar. It also concedes the uncomfortable part: containment failed in an environment built to contain.

Whether OpenAI names the paused models and when tool-use resumes.

Appeals court upholds the Pentagon’s “supply chain risk” label on Anthropic, 2–1

What happened

The D.C. Circuit ruled 2–1 on 25 September that the Department of War — the agency’s name in the opinion — acted within its authority in designating Anthropic a “supply chain risk,” a label that keeps the company out of military contracts. Judge Katsas wrote, Judge Rao joined, Judge Henderson dissented. The designation followed Anthropic’s refusal to permit Claude for autonomous weapons and mass surveillance. The court held that the Secretary’s concern about “overly constrained AI models shutting down unexpectedly and thus causing important military operations to fail” qualifies as a ‘supply chain risk’, and rejected Anthropic’s First Amendment claim. Anthropic said it respectfully disagrees and is considering its options.

What it means

The first appellate test of whether a US lab can keep its usage restrictions and still sell to the government, and the lab lost. Safety policy is now a procurement variable with a price attached.

Watch next

Whether Anthropic seeks review en banc or at the Supreme Court.

Microsoft moves Copilot off flat-rate pricing and rebuilds it around an agent

What happened

Microsoft restructured Copilot into three sections — Home, Code, and a new Autopilot agent — and shifted premium features to usage-based billing. Autopilot, built on OpenClaw, runs continuously in the cloud with its own workspace, storage and identity, can monitor Teams channels unattended, and is summoned by @mention in Teams, Outlook or a document. Jared Spataro, who runs Microsoft’s AI marketing, said it “uses Microsoft IQ to understand how an organization actually operates and includes permissions, auditing, and governance.” The standard licence now covers basic chat and Office integration only; Autopilot, Code and Cowork all meter.

What it means

Per-seat pricing assumed a human typing prompts. An agent that runs all night does not fit that model, and Microsoft has now said so with its price list rather than a blog post.

Whether the per-seat Copilot list price falls, and what Google and Salesforce do next.

Semiconductors

Rare earths, not chips, are the binding constraint in the China file

What happened

China’s rare-earth magnet shipments to the United States fell to 512 tonnes in August, down 20% from July and 13% year-on-year, according to figures carried by TrendForce. US heavy rare-earth output is expected to rise by 2031 but still fall short of domestic demand, leaving American manufacturers dependent on Chinese supply “well into the 2030s.” Washington’s position is that Beijing has not fully delivered on its commitment to keep supplies uninterrupted. The shortfall is worst in the heavy rare earths used in defence applications, despite public money into MP Materials and USA Rare Earth; MP has begun shipping magnets to General Motors. TrendForce is aggregating wire reporting we could not read directly, so treat the tonnage as second-hand.

What it means

The export-control conversation runs in both directions. Chips are what America withholds; magnets are what China withholds, and the substitution timeline for magnets is measured in years rather than quarters.

Micron closes its fiscal year on Wednesday, into the tightest memory market in years

What happened

Micron reports fiscal fourth-quarter results on Wednesday 30 September at 2:30pm Mountain time. One correction to carry: this is the fourth quarter and full year of fiscal 2026, not a first quarter — Micron’s year ends in late August. The set-up is unusually favourable: DRAM spot prices up 52% since January, 2026 HBM supply fully booked, HBM4 already shipping for Nvidia’s Vera Rubin, and Micron’s own end-of-life notice on both 2GB GDDR7 parts. The SOX semiconductor index closed Friday at 12,668.93, up 179.07 points, with the index page dated 25 September. August PCE lands the same morning.

What it means

Two of the three things that move semiconductor equities — an inflation print and the cleanest public read on memory pricing — arrive within six hours of each other. Memory is also the one part of the AI supply chain where pricing power has visibly moved to the seller.

Business

Anthropic buys $11.6bn of Akamai’s CPU cloud, and a warrant goes the other way

What happened

Akamai announced a seven-year, $11.6bn infrastructure agreement with Anthropic on 24 September, with an option for up to $9bn more — “a total potential commitment of approximately $20 billion” — the largest contract in its history. The capacity is CPU, not GPU, serving “Anthropic’s accelerating CPU workload demands.” Akamai will spend about $5.5bn over two years to build it, guides to $150–300m of 2027 revenue starting in the second half, and a $1.7bn annualised run-rate thereafter. Per the 8-K, Anthropic received a warrant convertible into 7,741,020 common shares — up to about 5% of Akamai — at a $111.33 common-equivalent strike, vesting 40% on Anthropic’s first payment and in 20% tranches per additional $3bn committed. Akamai rose about 15% on Friday.

What it means

A content-delivery network just became an AI infrastructure supplier, and took equity risk to win the business. For Anthropic it is a reminder that agents burn ordinary CPU, not only accelerators.

Akamai’s Q4 capex line, and whether the first $3bn tranche vests.

The long end sets a fresh high while the front end rallies

What happened

Treasury’s official par yield curve for Friday 25 September: the 30-year at 5.49%, up 2bp, and the 20-year at 5.54%, up 1bp — both fresh highs for this move. The 30-year has not printed at or above 5.49% since January 2002. The 10-year went the other way, easing a basis point to 5.17%; the 2-year fell 6bp to 4.81%, the 5-year 5bp to 4.98%. Several outlets reported a fresh 10-year high on Friday; Treasury’s data says it fell. Equities took it well: S&P 500 7,743.41 (+0.51%), Dow 51,828.62 (+0.93%), Nasdaq 27,068.72 (+0.48%), Russell 2000 2,837.55 (+0.07%).

What it means

A front-end rally with the long end still selling is a term-premium story, not a Fed story — the market repricing how much it wants to be paid to own duration, not when the next cut comes. The Russell’s flat close against the Dow’s 0.93% says the buying sat in a handful of megacaps.

Anthropic’s seven founders want 50.1% of the votes before the IPO

What happened

The Information reported on 25 September, relayed by TechCrunch, that Anthropic’s seven co-founders — Dario Amodei among them — are seeking special shares carrying 50.1% of the voting power on most corporate matters, conditional on at least three of them keeping minimum stakes. Each currently holds about 2% of the company, and the new shares carry no extra economic value. The Long-Term Benefit Trust would continue to select most of the board; founders’ board seats would rise from two to three; employees would receive stock that breaks ties on certain questions. Anthropic did not comment, and its newsroom carries nothing on governance. Reference points: a $965bn primary round in May and an August secondary-market valuation of $1.5 trillion, the latter from the same report. Single-origin — no company confirmation.

What it means

Dual-class control ahead of a listing is ordinary — Zuckerberg at Meta, Spiegel at Snap. Doing it as a bloc of seven, on top of a trust that already picks the board, is not. Read it as the founders expecting public-market pressure on precisely the safety commitments that cost them a Pentagon contract this week.

Watch next

Any S-1 filing, and whether the Long-Term Benefit Trust comments.

US

Supreme Court lets the expanded voter-verification database go live

What happened

On 25 September the Supreme Court stayed a district-court injunction in Department of Homeland Security v. League of Women Voters, application No. 26A308, over the dissents of Justices Sotomayor, Kagan and Jackson. The order clears the administration to use a modified SAVE system — Systematic Alien Verification for Entitlements — that now holds records of US-born citizens, reaches Social Security numbers and permits simultaneous bulk searches. Judge Sparkle Sooknanan blocked it on 22 June, finding breaches of federal privacy law, the Social Security Act and the Administrative Procedure Act; a divided D.C. Circuit panel declined to lift the injunction on 4 September. Jackson, writing for the three, said the harm of “burdening or disenfranchising even a few lawful voters outweighs the nonexistent harm that the Government experiences when it is prevented from taking an action that it likely lacks the authority to take”. No vote tally appears in the order and outlets differ on one, so we print none.

What it means

A stay is not a ruling on the merits, but with midterms on 3 November it is the practical outcome: the database will be in use for this election whatever the appeal eventually decides.

Fourteen senators ask Trump to un-invite Putin from the Miami G20

What happened

A bipartisan group of fourteen senators, led by Roger Wicker, who chairs Armed Services, and Jeanne Shaheen, ranking member on Foreign Relations, wrote to the President asking him to withdraw the G20 invitation extended to Vladimir Putin on 23 September. The letter warns that Putin’s attendance risks “legitimizing and normalizing a government that continues to attack” Ukrainian targets daily; seven senators from each party signed. The Washington Post reported it first. Otherwise Washington was quiet: the Senate held no roll-call votes on Friday — vote 244, the 49–50 Iran war powers resolution of the 24th, remains the session’s most recent — and the day’s only presidential action was the Gold Star Mother’s and Family’s Day proclamation.

What it means

Wicker’s signature is the substance here. An Armed Services chairman publicly contradicting the President over a summit guest list measures how little Republican cover the invitation has.

World Politics

Iran puts a seven-day clock on reopening the Strait of Hormuz

What happened

Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi laid a sequenced plan before the UN Security Council on 25 September, passed to Washington through Qatari mediation. “The seven-day timeline will start as soon as the United States accepts this plan,” he said: initial steps across four to five days, Hormuz reopens on day six, and “on day seven, the talks with the US will be started for the final deal on mutually agreed subjects” — the nuclear file. Iran wants the naval blockade lifted, oil-sales sanctions waived, roughly $12bn in frozen assets released, and a regional ceasefire covering Lebanon and Yemen. The strait previously carried a fifth of traded oil and gas. A similar memorandum in June collapsed within weeks over shipping-control terms.

What it means

This is Iran’s account of its own offer with no American answer attached — a negotiating position, not a schedule. But it is the first version with dates on it, and the oil market moved on it the same day.

Iran’s point-of-entry deadline on 27 September, and whether a second indirect round gets scheduled.

Germany cuts fuel duty for the second time in five months

What happened

The Bundestag passed a second Tankrabatt on Friday 25 September and the Bundesrat cleared it the same day, taking 14.04 cents a litre off the energy tax on petrol and diesel — about 17 cents gross once VAT is counted — from 1 October to 31 December. The cost is roughly EUR 2.5bn, split evenly between the federal government and the Länder, with no pass-through requirement. The first cut ran 1 May to 30 June and cost EUR 1.6bn. Green faction leader Katharina Dröge called it “a nonsensical solution”. Roll-call numbers were not obtainable for either chamber.

What it means

The Gulf is now setting German fiscal policy one quarter at a time. Two rebates in five months, neither carrying a pass-through requirement, is a government buying time rather than making policy.

Watch next

Pump prices on 1 October, and whether a third cut is floated for January.

Deep strikes both ways, and Kyiv’s toll rises to seven

What happened

The Ilsky refinery in Krasnodar Krai was reportedly struck by drones overnight into 26 September. Governor Veniamin Kondratiev reported fatalities, one person seriously injured and fires at two sites; the Russian independent outlet ASTRA analysed eyewitness footage. Ukraine did not claim it, and the Kyiv Independent could not verify that Ilsky was among the targets — one side’s account of its own territory is not confirmation. The plant sits about 500km from Ukrainian-held ground and has been hit at least sixteen times. Mayor Vitali Klitschko put Kyiv’s toll from Thursday night at seven dead, including the 14-year-old, with 59 injured.

What it means

Ukraine’s refinery campaign reaches Russian revenue without waiting on Washington. Russia’s answer is tempo against cities, and the exchange rate in civilian lives does not favour Kyiv.
