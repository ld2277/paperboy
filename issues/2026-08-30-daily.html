AI

Sony and Warner sue Anthropic — and name Amodei and Mann personally

What happened

Sony Music Publishing and Warner Chappell filed suit against Anthropic PBC, chief executive Dario Amodei and co-founder Benjamin Mann in the Northern District of California, San Jose Division, on Friday 28 August, case no. 5:26-cv-09217. The complaint opens by alleging the defendants "have conducted a brazen campaign of illegally torrenting, scraping, and downloading copyrighted works on a massive scale in order to develop, operate, and reap enormous profits from Anthropic's 'Claude' series" of models, and says Claude was trained on "tens of thousands" of the publishers' compositions — lyrics and sheet music — listed in an exhibit. The publishers seek up to $150,000 per work for wilful infringement plus up to $25,000 for each removal of copyright-management information. Anthropic had not commented as of Sunday morning. Its $1.5bn settlement with authors in Bartz v. Anthropic — agreed September 2025, given final approval in July 2026 — is still the largest copyright settlement in US history. (complaint)

What it means

Naming the founders individually is the aggressive move here, and it is deliberate: it makes the torrenting allegation about decisions people made rather than a corporate practice, and it is much harder to settle quietly. Music publishing is also a worse defendant's beat than books — compositions are registered, catalogued and easy to enumerate, so the exhibit does the work that discovery had to do in Bartz. The same legal team filed for Concord and UMG in January.

A survey puts AI job displacement at 3% so far — with a hole in the middle of it

What happened

A YouGov online survey of 1,250 employed US adults, fielded 30 July to 4 August, found 3% had lost a job to AI since 2023, while 6% got a job AI created and 9% got an AI-related promotion; 95% reported no AI-driven job loss. The write-up is by Jeffrey C. Dixon, a sociologist at the College of the Holy Cross, published via The Conversation: "AI has not yet resulted in substantial job losses or gains for these workers, contrary to what some AI pessimists and optimists might have predicted." (Fortune)

What it means

Dixon flags the flaw himself and it is fatal to the headline number: the sample is employed adults, which structurally excludes anyone AI displaced who has not found new work — precisely the population the question is about. Read it as evidence that AI has not visibly reorganised the jobs of people still in jobs, which is a smaller and duller claim than "3%".

Semiconductors

The Korean memory supercycle turns up in the tax receipts

What happened

Samsung Electronics and SK hynix paid a combined 11.2 trillion won ($8.1bn) in corporate tax in the first half of 2026, up 167% year on year — the second-highest first-half total on record behind 15.63 trillion won in H1 2019, according to Yonhap's reading of the Financial Supervisory Service filings repository. The split was 3.99tn won for Samsung and 7.22tn won for SK hynix. Separately confirmed against quarterly filings: H1 2026 operating profit was 146.7tn won for Samsung (against 11.4tn a year earlier) and 98.2tn won for SK hynix (against 16.7tn). Both are forecast to post full-year operating profit of 385tn and 266tn won respectively. (Korea Times)

What it means

SK hynix paying nearly twice Samsung's tax on two-thirds of the operating profit looks wrong until you notice Korean firms settle the prior year in March — the H1 bill mostly reflects 2025, when SK hynix actually out-earned Samsung on operating profit. The real signal is the 167%: memory pricing has moved enough to show up in a sovereign's revenue line, which is about as hard a confirmation of the shortage as exists.

Dell's fiscal Q2 on 1 September — the first large buyer to talk about memory costs since the last round of contract settlements.

The government's chip stakes are up 300%, and now they have a legal problem

What happened

Fortune tallied the administration's equity positions and their paper returns: Intel "soared over 300%" in the year since the initial report of stake talks; MP Materials up 87% since the Pentagon's $400m equity investment in July 2025; Trilogy Metals up 73% since the US took a 10% stake in October 2025. A shareholder suit naming Intel's board, the Department of Commerce and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick seeks to unwind the government's position, arguing the CHIPS Act gives no authority to demand equity as a condition of receiving a grant. "There is a sort of interventionist approach that is not fully litigated and mediated in the American system yet," said Matt Gertken of BCA Research. (Fortune)

What it means

The returns are the reason nobody has pushed hard on the legal question yet — it is difficult to sue over a policy that tripled your holding. The suit is the first real test of whether the equity-stake model survives contact with a statute that was written to hand out grants, and a ruling against it would reach every deal built the same way.

Jackson Hole ended quietly — the Saturday papers are posted and nobody covered them

What happened

The Kansas City Fed's symposium closed on Saturday 29 August with a session chaired by Anil Kashyap of Chicago: Christine Parlour (Berkeley) on financial innovation and the future of banking with Itay Goldstein discussing; Markus Brunnermeier (Princeton) on financing innovation with Raghuram Rajan discussing; and a central-banking panel of Wenxin Du (Harvard), Arvind Krishnamurthy (Stanford) and Jesús Fernández-Villaverde (Penn). All papers and handouts are posted. No outlet wrote up any of it. (KC Fed)

What it means

This is the normal shape of Jackson Hole — the press corps files on the chair's Friday keynote and goes home, and the academic work the symposium exists to produce reaches nobody. Worth knowing if you want the actual argument on stablecoins and payments rather than the market reaction to it: the PDFs are the only route, and they are free.

US

Milo Yiannopoulos deported to the UK after an ICE arrest in New Orleans

What happened

Yiannopoulos was arrested by ICE at Louis Armstrong New Orleans International Airport on Thursday for overstaying a visa he entered on in May 2019, and was flown to the UK on Friday 28 August. An immigration judge had issued a removal order on 22 July after he failed to appear at a hearing. "He chose to overstay his welcome in violation of our nation's laws," a DHS spokesperson said; the department posted his booking photo alongside its standing offer of $3,000 and a free flight to self-deport. DHS did not say what visa he originally held. (AP via KSAT)

What it means

The seven-year overstay is the detail that matters: it is the kind of case that sat unenforced for most of a decade and is now being worked, publicised with a mugshot, and used as advertising for the self-deportation programme. Enforcement as messaging, aimed at an audience rather than at him.

Whether he contests the removal from the UK — a removal order after a missed hearing is reopenable.

The groundwork for contesting midterm results is being laid now

What happened

It lands in a week already dense with election litigation: the combined preliminary-injunction hearing in the consolidated mail-voting cases is Wednesday 3 September, and North Carolina mails its first absentee ballots Friday 4 September — one day of slack. What it means

World Politics

Nepal–Tibet flood: 750 dead, more than 3,000 missing, and 100 men possibly alive in tunnels

What happened

Nepal's disaster authority put the toll at 734 dead, 2,498 missing and 242 injured in its 9am Sunday update, up from 633 dead on Friday. Chinese authorities separately report 16 dead and 546 missing in Tibet's Gyirong County, including 261 foreigners from 23 countries — missing on the Tibetan side, not in Nepal. 933 of the missing are hydropower workers, and Nepal believes more than 100 may still be alive inside tunnels at the Trishuli 3A and 3B sites in Rasuwa; the army drilled in on Saturday, pumped air and inserted cameras. Losses are put above $4bn. The barrier lake has drained — China's water ministry says the 2 million cubic metres impounded behind Wednesday 26 August's glacier collapse on Langtang Ri "had been almost completely discharged" — but the Bhotekoshi and Trishuli rose again on Sunday. (Kathmandu Post, Al Jazeera)

What it means

The missing count is now more than three times the dead, and on a flood of this kind that usually resolves downward into the death toll rather than into rescues. The tunnels are the exception, and the reason this is still a rescue: sealed, above the water line, with air. The Tibet figures are Chinese state announcements, not independently verified.

Watch next

The tunnel drilling at Trishuli 3A, the river levels downstream, and whether the missing count starts converting.

Thirty-seven dead at a Ukrainian ammunition depot, and Ukraine is prosecuting its own

What happened

A Russian drone hit an ammunition storage site at Myla, Kyiv Oblast, 5km west of the capital, at 8:10pm on 28 August. The toll rose through Saturday to 37 killed and 42 injured, including four children; Taras Didych, head of the Dmytrivka community, died responding. Around 50 homes and a care facility were damaged, 381 people evacuated. Ukraine has opened criminal proceedings for official negligence. Zelensky: "There are regulations: ammunition cannot be stored near houses or other residential buildings." Prime Minister Serhii Koretskyi: "On the fifth year of the full-scale invasion, making the same mistakes is criminal negligence." Russia struck Kyiv for a third straight night into Sunday. Separately, Axios reports — two anonymous sources, unmatched elsewhere — that CIA director John Ratcliffe floated a Trump–Putin–Zelensky trilateral in Moscow on Tuesday 25 August. (Kyiv Independent, Axios)

What it means

A government prosecuting its own officials over where a depot was sited, three days after the strike, is politically deliberate: it converts a Russian success into a domestic accountability story before anyone else frames it. Quotes are the Kyiv Independent's translation; Al Jazeera renders both slightly differently.

Guinea-Bissau votes to shrink its own parliament

What happened

Polls opened Sunday 30 August on a constitution that expands presidential powers, cuts parliament from 102 to 65 seats and constituencies from 29 to 12, and creates a Constitutional Court. Roughly 915,000 voters are registered; the PAIGC is boycotting. Armed forces chief General Tomás Djassi backed the change: "In a community, there is only one leader... the president of the republic is the national symbol." The vote precedes December elections meant to restore civilian rule after the coup of November 2025; transitional president General Horta Inta-A Na Man is barred from standing. No turnout figures as of this morning. (Al Jazeera)

What it means

A referendum held under a transitional military government, boycotted by the largest opposition party, on a text that concentrates power in the office the same government will hand over in December. Whatever the result, its legitimacy is the contested thing, not its arithmetic.

Turnout, the result, and whether the PAIGC boycotts December too.
