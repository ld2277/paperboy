AI

Anthropic opens at $111.50, closes at $97.17

What happened

Anthropic began trading on the Nasdaq Global Select Market as “ANT” on Thursday. The first trade came out of Nasdaq’s opening auction at $111.50 at 11:48 a.m. ET, two hours and eighteen minutes after the bell. It touched $115.10 a minute later, bottomed at $92.72 at 1:18 p.m. and closed at $97.17 — up 17.1% on the $83.00 offer price, down 12.9% from its own first print. On the 13,219,642,276 shares of all classes the final prospectus says will be outstanding after the offering and the concurrent private placement, the close puts the company at about $1.28 trillion; on a fully diluted basis it is nearer $1.75 trillion. Reported volume was about 832 million shares, from one venue.

What it means

Two different stories are being told with the same number. Measured against the price institutions paid, the deal worked. Measured against where the stock actually opened to everyone else, Thursday was a 13% decline — and the $81.34bn of gross proceeds is fixed at $83 a share whatever the stock does, so it cannot be compared with the pop. A first day that gives back most of its gain is the market saying the book was priced about right and the auction was not.

Watch next

No 8-K reporting exercise of the 147,000,000-share over-allotment option or the closing of the offering had been filed as of Thursday night; settlement was Friday and the filing can come up to four business days later.

House committee publishes the China AI-chip licence records

What happened

On Thursday the chairman of the House Foreign Affairs Committee, Brian Mast, used authority under the Export Control Reform Act to make public normally confidential export-licensing records. The committee’s own release says more than 879,000 advanced AI chips were approved for Chinese buyers against 3.4 million applied for, and names Alibaba and Tencent among recipients designated by the Department of War as Chinese military companies — without giving volumes. The per-company figures circulating (Tencent 150,000, Alibaba 75,000, Baidu 43,000, BYD 4,000), the December 2025 to May 2026 window and the Nvidia H200 and AMD MI325X model names are Reuters’ reporting, not the committee’s. The underlying records are posted only as embedded documents we could not read.

What it means

The number is the point: roughly a quarter of what was requested got through, which is neither the blockade the hawks describe nor the open door the China sceptics fear. Publishing it at all is the shift — licensing decisions that were deliberately opaque are now a congressional oversight lever, and the committee did it the week Commerce rewrote the rules.

Watch next

Whether Commerce responds; it has said nothing. The new remote-access rule’s comment docket closes 18 January 2027.

Semiconductors

TSMC suspends Chinese designers’ orders at 16nm and below

What happened

TSMC has suspended previously permitted advanced-node orders from Chinese chip designers after this week’s Commerce rule removed the route it had relied on, under which the foundry itself reviewed a customer’s design to decide whether a licence exemption applied. Affected orders at 16nm or smaller are halted unless the customer obtains a US export licence, per Taiwan News, aggregating Reuters (two people familiar with the matter), the Financial Times and CNA. TSMC itself said only that it was aware of the rules and would comply with all applicable laws and regulations, and did not comment on the reports. Without a licence, Chinese customers are described as limited to 28nm and 40nm and barred from high-bandwidth memory and advanced packaging; the reported list of approved designers runs Nvidia, AMD, Apple, MediaTek and Realtek, with no Chinese companies on it.

What it means

This is the rule biting inside a week, and it bites at the foundry rather than the customer — which is the whole design of it. The exemption TSMC lost was a self-certification: Washington has replaced a supplier’s judgement with its own paperwork. Xiaomi, Oppo and Vivo are the names flagged for delays, which puts a chip-export rule into phone supply chains.

Watch next

Whether any Chinese designer files for a licence, and whether Samsung needs the same cover.

Asia’s chip trade has its second brutal session

What happened

Taiwan’s TAIEX closed Friday at 36,033.29, down 2,393.60 points or 6.23% on turnover of NT$877.4bn, a day after falling 4.40%; TSMC lost NT$100 to NT$2,000, down 4.76%. The KOSPI fell 267.93 to 4,869.96, down 5.21%, with program trading briefly suspended and foreigners selling a net 1.13 trillion won; Samsung Electronics closed at 183,100 won, down 7.01%, and SK Hynix at 1,113,000 won, down 5.92%, having risen 3.41% the day before. The Nikkei 225 fell 3,007.56 to 56,436.65, down 5.06%, and the TOPIX 3.88%. Hong Kong and the mainland were spared the worst — Hang Seng down 1.89%, Shanghai Composite down 1.22% — but Hang Seng Tech fell 3.78% and ChiNext 3.54%.

What it means

The damage is sectoral, not national: wherever an index is heavy in AI hardware it fell five or six per cent, and wherever it is not it fell one. Samsung Securities put it on “Concerns over the Federal Reserve’s possible tightening and continued foreign selling”, which is the honest answer — nobody named a trigger. The Taiwanese “worst week since 1991” line doing the rounds comes from a wire we cannot read and Focus Taiwan does not say it.

Business

Wall Street drops 2%, and December is now 99% priced for a hike

What happened

Thursday’s closes, from the AP finals: Dow 49,097.06, down 1,101.11 or 2.19%; S&P 500 7,175.83, down 147.80 or 2.02%; Nasdaq Composite 23,879.20, down 550.69 or 2.25%; Russell 2000 2,655.11, down 59.20 or 2.18%. Treasury yields fell across the curve on the Treasury’s own par curve — two-year 4.76%, ten-year 5.04%, thirty-year 5.40%, down four, three and one basis point. December WTI settled at $83.21, down 0.06%, on its last day as front month; the more active January contract settled at $82.44, up 0.2%, and January Brent at $91.98, up 0.03%. Rate futures put a quarter-point increase at 99%, a larger move at 1% and a hold at nothing.

What it means

Equities down two per cent while bonds rally and a hike goes to near-certainty is not a growth scare — it is a repricing of one trade. The money leaving semiconductors is going into Treasuries, not out of risk altogether. Vice Chair Jefferson, speaking in Brasília, stayed conditional: “If inflation expectations risk becoming unanchored, a stronger reaction to inflation is warranted.” That is not a signal, and the market is not waiting for one.

Walmart raises again, but the US shopper slowed by half

What happened

Walmart reported the quarter ended 31 October at 7 a.m. ET Thursday: revenue of $188.8bn, up 5.2% or 4.5% in constant currency, GAAP earnings of $0.62 a share and adjusted earnings of $0.65 against its own $0.62 to $0.64 guide. Operating income rose 16.0% as reported but 5.5% adjusted in constant currency. Walmart US comparable sales excluding fuel rose 2.9%, against 4.5% in the same quarter last year; global eCommerce grew 17% and membership and other income $2,078m, up 20.3%. Full-year adjusted earnings guidance went to $2.84–$2.89 from the $2.80–$2.87 set in August, itself raised from $2.75–$2.85 in February. The release says the timing of Flipkart’s Big Billion Days moved about 500 basis points of international sales growth out of the quarter, with a corresponding benefit in the fourth.

What it means

Both halves are real and they point opposite ways. The raise is the second of the year and the eCommerce and membership lines are genuinely strong. But the core American comparable halved, the headline operating-income growth is three times the underlying rate once currency and one-offs come out, and a chunk of the international number is a calendar shift that will flatter next quarter instead. No fourth-quarter guidance was given, and tariffs appear nowhere in the release except the risk list.

Watch next

The fourth quarter, reporting in February, which carries the Flipkart benefit and so will overstate international growth in the other direction.

Three more retailers, and the tariff refunds now run both ways

What happened

Gap reported net sales of $4.0bn, up 2%, with comparable sales flat, GAAP earnings of $0.86 a share and adjusted earnings of $0.82 — the adjusted figure is the lower one because it strips out the tariff recovery. It cut full-year sales growth to roughly flat from 1% to 1.5% and adjusted earnings to $2.29–$2.34 from $2.35–$2.45; Athleta’s comparable sales fell 15% while the Gap brand rose 9%. Williams-Sonoma showed the same inversion — revenue $1,991.2m, up 5.8%, comparable brand revenue up 5.1%, GAAP earnings $2.38 against non-GAAP $2.19 — and reiterated its guidance. Ross Stores went the other way: sales $6.3bn, up 12%, comparable sales up 8%, earnings $1.91 on a GAAP-only basis, and a full-year raise to $8.75–$8.84 that its own release says includes about $0.60 of tariff refunds booked in the second quarter.

What it means

The refunds have made the two earnings measures swap places, and the direction tells you where the money sits. At Gap and Williams-Sonoma the adjusted number is lower because the refund is being excluded; at Ross and Target it is inside the raised full-year figure. Any sentence calling these improvements in the business is wrong unless it names the refund, and Gap is the one with a real problem — a guidance cut, a flat comparable and a brand down 15%.

Fourth-quarter reports in late February, the first quarters without the refund distortion.

Startups

Isomorphic Labs raises $5bn — and we were three days late to it

What happened

Alphabet’s AI drug-design subsidiary announced a $5 billion Series C on 17 November, co-led by Greenoaks and Sequoia Capital with Andreessen Horowitz and other new investors joining, Alphabet continuing to invest, and every existing backer participating — Thrive Capital, MGX, GV, CapitalG, Temasek and the UK Sovereign AI Fund. No valuation was disclosed, on either a pre- or post-money basis. The money goes to the company’s IsoDDE drug-design engine and a pipeline of AI-designed therapeutic candidates. The announcement names no specific candidate, no trial and no timeline. It falls outside this brief’s window and we are reporting it late.

What it means

Five billion dollars with no disclosed valuation is a deliberate choice, and so is the absence of a named clinical candidate. The round is a bet on the design engine rather than on any molecule — which is the AlphaFold thesis taken to its logical and very expensive conclusion. A company that cannot yet point to a human trial raising this much is a statement about how patient the capital behind AI-for-biology has become.

The first named clinical candidate, and whether any of the pipeline reaches a trial.

ESS Tech liquidates and fires everyone the same day

What happened

ESS Tech, the iron-flow battery maker, filed a voluntary Chapter 7 petition in the US Bankruptcy Court for the District of Delaware on 13 November and, effective the same day, terminated all employees and officers without cause — including chief executive Drew Buckley, chief financial officer Kate Suhadolnik, who signed the filing, and general counsel Kelly Goodman. A trustee now controls the assets. This is liquidation, not reorganisation. The filing names no dollar amounts and no case number, and the cover page lists the exchange for its stock as “NONE”, so it was already delisted. No new venture round dated 19 or 20 November surfaced anywhere we looked.

What it means

Chapter 7 rather than Chapter 11 means nobody thought the business was worth saving as a going concern — there is no plan, no debtor in possession, just a trustee selling what is there. Long-duration storage has been the part of the energy transition that could never get its cost curve to move, and a public company reaching liquidation with its officers dismissed on the day of filing is the market making that judgement unambiguous.

US

Senate rejects an Iran war-powers resolution, 48–51

What happened

At 2:01 p.m. Thursday the Senate rejected H.Con.Res. 93 by 48 to 51, with Senator Murphy not voting. The measure is described in the vote record as “A concurrent resolution directing the President, pursuant to section 5(c) of the War Powers Resolution, to remove United States Armed Forces from hostilities with Iran.” It was the chamber’s only recorded vote of the day, and nothing has been recorded for Friday. H.R. 2347 — the Survivor Justice Tax Prevention Act, which excludes from gross income non-punitive damages received on account of sexual acts or contact — got cloture 65 to 31 on Wednesday and has had no vote since; cloture is not passage. The House vote sheet for Thursday lists a single item, H.R. 4464, and prints no tally.

What it means

Democrats hold this chamber, so a three-vote loss on pulling forces out of hostilities with Iran was not a party-line result — enough of the majority either voted against it or stayed out of the way. That is the clearest read available on how much congressional appetite exists to constrain the Gulf campaign, and the answer is: not quite enough. The procedural history piled onto H.R. 2347 — a budget waiver, a substitute amendment — suggests a bill being used as a vehicle for something else.

Factory sentiment collapses while mortgage rates jump

What happened

The Philadelphia Fed’s November manufacturing survey put current general activity at 6.3, down from 20.9 in October and a six-month low. New orders turned negative at −0.4 from 4.3, the lowest since May; prices paid jumped 15 points to 58.2, the highest since April; shipments rose to 25.7 and employment to 11.6. Six-month capital-expenditure expectations fell to 18.9 from 34.9. All figures are seasonally adjusted diffusion indexes. Initial jobless claims for the week ending 14 November were 203,000, with the prior week revised up to 202,000 and the four-week average at 197,750; continuing claims for the week to 7 November rose 70,000 to 1,763,000. Freddie Mac’s Thursday survey put the 30-year fixed mortgage at 7.28%, up from 7.11%, and the 15-year at 6.66% from 6.45%.

What it means

Orders negative and prices paid at a seven-month high is the shape of the problem the Fed has been describing: demand softening while costs do not. The 70,000 jump in continuing claims matters more than the steady initial number — people are not losing jobs faster, they are staying unemployed longer. And a 17 basis point jump in the mortgage rate in a week, reversing the previous week’s fall, is the hike getting priced into households.

World Politics

The Shenzhen declaration has a text, and Ukraine is not in it

What happened

Correcting ourselves: we reported that no government had published the three-way declaration agreed in Shenzhen. Both have. Xinhua put out “Full text: China-Russia-U.S. Heads of State Declaration toward Peace” on Thursday, and the White House published the same document as “U.S.-Russia-China Heads of State Declaration toward Peace”. The four points are identical: UN Security Council and UN reform; a plan to “increase the production and supply of refined petroleum products”; a statement that the principle of state consent matters and that “the International Criminal Court and other similar bodies must not abuse their jurisdiction”; and a reaffirmation that “a nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought”, with a dialogue on nuclear risk. Each government names its own president first, and each gives Xi Jinping a different title — “State Chairman” at the White House, “President” at Xinhua. The word “Ukraine” does not appear anywhere in it.

What it means

A three-way summit convened in the middle of a European land war produced a document that does not mention the war. What it does mention is instructive: oil supply, and a joint warning to the International Criminal Court from two leaders who are subject to its warrants. There is no signature block, no deadline and no enforcement — the verbs are “plan to increase”, “will conduct a dialogue”, “reaffirmed” and “concurred”. Xinhua does not even name an issuing body.

Watch next

Whether the refined-products clause produces anything measurable, and whether any of the three publishes an implementing text.

The largest combined strike of the war, and an object lands in Poland

What happened

Ukraine’s Air Force says the attack began at 18:00 Thursday and was still running at 10:30 Friday, when it published a preliminary count: 84 air- and sea-launched cruise missiles, Iskander-M, S-400 and Zircon missiles with no launch figure given, and 404 Shahed-type drones of which 123 were jet-powered, plus uncounted decoys. It claims 462 targets neutralised — three ballistic, 79 cruise and 380 drones — with impacts at 23 locations and debris at ten more. These are a belligerent’s figures for its own operations, posted to its own channel. At least eight were killed: six dead and 15 injured in Kyiv, per Prime Minister Serhiy Koretskyi, superseding Mayor Klitschko’s earlier count of three; two dead and ten injured in Lviv, per Mayor Andriy Sadovyi, a figure that rose through the morning. In Poland, an object came down near Chmielek in Biłgoraj county and another at Nowy Lipowiec; Prime Minister Tusk confirmed a violation of Polish airspace shortly before 8 a.m. and later said there was no current air threat.

What it means

Four hundred drones and eighty-four cruise missiles in one night, the day after a summit about peace, is the answer to what the declaration was worth. The Polish piece is the part to watch rather than the tonnage: Warsaw has now confirmed its airspace was violated, which is a NATO member stating a fact it cannot easily un-state, and it has not said what caused the explosion.

Watch next

Poland’s radar findings. A later headline we could not fetch reports the defence minister describing two missiles and a drone hitting Polish territory; that is unverified, and Warsaw’s account was escalating through Friday morning.

Berlin produces six conditions and no force

What happened

Leaders of close to 40 countries met at the Chancellery on Thursday, hours after Shenzhen, in a meeting co-hosted by Germany, France and the UK and convened to discuss a multinational force for Ukraine. No communiqué was issued and no force numbers emerged. Chancellor Merz instead set out six European conditions: talks involving Ukraine, Russia, the US and Europe; Ukrainian sovereignty preserved; firm security guarantees after a ceasefire; the current line of contact as the starting point for territorial agreements; a continued EU path and reconstruction aid; and Germany, France, the UK, Poland and Italy speaking for Europe. He floated the possible gradual lifting of sanctions under conditions, and called the Shenzhen summit a “photo op”. Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff attended and briefed partners on China.

What it means

The meeting’s stated purpose produced nothing on the record, which is itself the finding — thirty-odd governments can agree on principles and cannot agree on troops. Merz’s fourth condition is the substantive one: accepting the current line of contact as a starting point concedes territory as a negotiating premise, which is further than European leaders have previously gone in public.

Watch next

Kushner said on Friday, of a guarantee modelled on NATO’s Article 5, “I think we’ve gotten the Russians comfortable with that concept.” That is a US account of a Russian position, from one outlet, with no Russian corroboration.
