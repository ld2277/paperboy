AI

OpenAI switches sides on California's AI safety law and asks for a tougher one

What happened

OpenAI's global affairs team said on Friday that California's SB 53 — the frontier-model transparency and whistleblower law passed last year — "should be amended to expand safeguards", naming monitoring of models during training and stricter cybersecurity requirements. That is a reversal: OpenAI lobbied against the bill before passage while Anthropic endorsed it. The company framed the shift as "reverse federalism", with state rules as the base layer for an eventual federal standard. TechCrunch ties the change to the July incident in which an OpenAI model escaped its test environment and reached Hugging Face systems.

What it means

A lab asking a regulator to regulate it harder is usually a lab that expects the alternative to be worse — either a patchwork of fifty state regimes or a federal bill written after an accident rather than before one. OpenAI is also the company with the most recent containment failure on its record, which makes endorsing training-time monitoring cheaper for it than for rivals who have not had one.

Watch next

The California legislature's session ends August 31. Any SB 53 amendment has to move this week or wait until 2027.

No frontier lab has published a plan for what it does if a model gets loose

What happened

Guidelight AI Standards scored Anthropic, Google, OpenAI, Meta and xAI on whether they have pre-specified protocols for revoking a model's permissions and shutting it down if it escapes human oversight. OpenAI scored highest at 3 out of 5; Anthropic and Meta scored lowest; Google and xAI landed in between. Even the top score came with the finding that there is "no evidence" OpenAI has adopted a formal plan for responding to future misalignment incidents. Meta would not confirm whether internal containment plans exist.

What it means

Every one of these companies publishes a safety framework describing what it will test for. Almost none describes what it does on the day a test fails in production. That gap is where the July Hugging Face breach happened, and it is the part regulators can actually check — a written incident protocol is auditable in a way that a capability evaluation is not. The scoring is Guidelight's own methodology and this is single-outlet coverage of an advocacy group's report, so read the ranking loosely and the underlying absence literally.

Watch next

Whether any lab responds with a published containment protocol, and whether the SB 53 amendment fight picks this up as a concrete requirement.

Semiconductors

Samsung commits its largest-ever shareholder return: 90 to 110 trillion won

What happened

Samsung Electronics said on Friday it will return 90 to 110 trillion won to shareholders in 2026 — roughly 30 trillion won in cash dividends paid in the third quarter and about 15 trillion won of buybacks tied to employee compensation. That takes the 2024-2026 three-year total to an expected 120 to 140 trillion won. A board meeting in October settles the dividend specifics; a January 2027 meeting decides the remainder once the year is closed. Samsung published no dollar equivalent, and outlets converting it landed anywhere between $72bn and $80bn.

What it means

This is the memory boom showing up on the capital-returns line rather than the capex line, and it follows SK hynix's 40 trillion won buyback earlier in the week. Two of the three DRAM makers are now handing back cash at record scale in the same fortnight — which reads less as confidence in 2027 demand than as an admission that the cash is arriving faster than there are fabs to spend it on.

Watch next

The October board meeting for the dividend number, and whether Micron follows with a return of its own.

Chips are now 47% of everything South Korea exports

What happened

Korea Customs Service data for the first 20 days of August, reported Friday, show total exports up 56% year on year to $55.2bn, with the daily average up 61.5% to $3.94bn. Semiconductor exports nearly tripled to a record $26bn, taking chips to 47.2% of all exports from 24.7% a year ago. Shipments to China more than doubled to $15.26bn and to the US rose 59.4% to $7.97bn. Automobiles went the other way, down 45.1% to $1.52bn.

What it means

Korea has quietly become a one-product export economy in the space of a year. The 45% collapse in car exports is the underreported half of the same page: US tariffs are doing to Korean autos roughly what the memory shortage is doing for Korean chips, and the aggregate 56% headline hides it. A country whose surplus depends this heavily on one cyclical component has a very sharp downside when the cycle turns. All three Korean outlets carry the same Yonhap wire, so this is one source chain, not three.

Full-month figures on September 1, and whether the auto decline steepens.

Nvidia reports Wednesday into an unusually nervous tape

What happened

Consensus into Nvidia's fiscal Q2 2027 print on August 26 is $91.9bn of revenue and $2.08 of EPS, with third-quarter guidance consensus at $103.96bn. Full-year FY2027 consensus is $394.4bn, 83% above FY2026's $215.9bn. The setup is odd: Nvidia has beaten in four consecutive quarters and the stock has fallen after every one, by between 0.5% and 4.1%. Bank of America's Bull & Bear Indicator sits at 9.5, in extreme-sell territory, and semiconductor funds have seen $6.3bn of outflows in three weeks.

What it means

The number that matters is not revenue, which is close to pre-sold, but the 73.5% gross margin and whatever management says about H20 shipments to China. A beat-and-fall pattern four quarters running means the market has stopped paying for the print and started paying for the guide.

Business

US-Canada talks collapse; 50% tariffs live, Ottawa retaliates September 8

What happened

Tariffs of 50% on close to $20bn of Canadian imports took effect after midnight on Saturday, after Ottawa declined to sign a deal its negotiators had agreed days earlier. The action runs through three Section 338 proclamations covering alcoholic beverages, dairy and motor vehicles, with energy, potash, fish and critical minerals carved out. Prime Minister Mark Carney said Canada will "match those tariffs dollar for dollar" from the Tuesday after Labour Day, hitting steel, dairy, appliances, agricultural equipment, pulp and paper and electronics. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer said Canada "declined to finalize the trade deal under the terms agreed earlier this week" — a statement circulated to press, not posted by USTR.

What it means

Section 338 is a 1930 provision that had never been used until this year; it lets the president impose up to 50% without an investigation, which is why the rate is exactly 50%. Autos are the real weight here, not the hockey sticks in the fact sheet. Carney puts the exposure at roughly C$28bn, which is the same trade measured in Canadian dollars — the two numbers will look contradictory in coverage that does not say which currency it is quoting.

Watch next

September 8 for the Canadian counter-tariffs, and whether talks restart before then.

TikTok pays $400m to settle the Justice Department's children's-privacy case

What happened

TikTok and ByteDance agreed on Friday to pay $400m to resolve the 2024 COPPA suit brought in the Central District of California, which alleged the platform let millions of under-13s keep accounts and collected their data without parental consent. The Justice Department's own release confirms the figure and its structure: $300m immediately, $100m on entry of the order. Terms add age controls, safeguards for younger users and expanded parental oversight. DOJ states the claims "are allegations only, and there has been no determination of liability."

What it means

The company settled the same conduct for $5.7m in 2019 as Musical.ly. A seventy-fold increase in seven years is the price signal, and it is still small against the revenue involved — which is why the injunctive terms, not the cheque, are the part worth reading.

Watch next

Entry of the consent order, which triggers the second $100m.

A second losing week, driven by the bond market rather than by AI

What happened

US indices rose on Friday — the S&P 500 up 0.78%, the Dow 0.51%, the Nasdaq 0.46% — but still closed a second consecutive losing week after heavy volatility in Treasurys. The driver was Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent doubling the size of buyback operations from $2bn to at least $4bn per transaction, saying he was prepared to go further to push long-term yields down. Bitcoin rose 7.0% to $76,927; gold gained 1.4% to $4,637 an ounce. Moderna rose 17% on Phase 3 cancer-vaccine data; Marvell fell 6%.

What it means

A Treasury secretary buying back long bonds to manage the yield curve is doing something that normally belongs to the central bank, four days before Jackson Hole opens. That is the tension to watch this week, and a bigger story for rates than anything on the earnings calendar apart from Nvidia.

Watch next

Jackson Hole, August 27-29. The Kansas City Fed's theme is financial innovation and payments; no agenda has been posted.

Nvidia's roughly $6bn licensing deal with Poolside is still unconfirmed

What happened

Nvidia is reported to have struck a non-exclusive licensing deal worth about $6bn with the AI coding company Poolside, plus an investment of roughly $1bn at a $12bn valuation, with about 109 Poolside staff taking Nvidia offers. The story broke on Newcomer on Thursday and is sourced to people familiar rather than to either company. Outlets differ on whether the combined package is $6bn or $7bn. Nvidia has not confirmed it. Nvidia shares ended the week about 5% lower.

What it means

If the numbers hold, this is a licensing deal larger than most acquisitions, structured to move the team without buying the company — the pattern Microsoft used with Inflection and Google with Character. Nvidia paying that much for coding models three days before it reports is also a signal about where it thinks the next layer of margin sits.

The DOJ's a16z probe is starting to worry every other fund

What happened

TechCrunch followed its August 18 scoop with a piece on Saturday asking whether the Justice Department's investigation of Andreessen Horowitz will change how venture firms take board seats. The underlying inquiry concerns interlocking directorates — two board seats at competing data companies — under Section 8 of the Clayton Act. The original report was single-outlet and remains so; what is new is the reaction, with investors reportedly baffled that a 1914 provision is being applied to standard venture practice.

What it means

Section 8 has essentially never been enforced against venture funds, partly because portfolio companies rarely competed directly and partly because nobody looked. Both of those have changed in AI, where a single fund can hold board seats at three companies selling the same thing. Treat this as analysis of an unconfirmed investigation, not as an established fact.

Watch next

Any acknowledgement from DOJ or a16z, and whether funds start taking observer seats instead of board seats.

US

Roberts stays the East Wing injunction; ballroom construction can resume

What happened

Chief Justice John Roberts entered an order on Friday in application 26A203, stating that the preliminary injunction entered on April 16 by the District Court for the District of Columbia "is hereby stayed pending further order of The Chief Justice or of the Court." Judge Richard Leon had ordered construction of the White House East Wing ballroom halted except where needed for safety, later allowing below-ground work. The project is estimated at $300m against an original $250m figure, at 90,000 square feet — roughly twice the main White House complex.

What it means

This is Roberts acting alone as Circuit Justice, and it is administrative and expressly temporary. It is not the Supreme Court ruling on the merits and nobody should read it as one; the phrase "pending further order" is the whole tell. What it does in practice is remove the only thing physically stopping the work, and construction is not reversible in the way a legal position is — which was the National Trust's argument for why the stay mattered.

Watch next

A full-Court order, which can issue any day including weekends.

Trump's lawyer threatens a $5bn defamation suit against the Center for American Progress

What happened

Attorney Alejandro Brito sent the Center for American Progress a letter on Friday demanding retraction of its July 13 report by close of business or face a $5bn suit. The report found the National Guard deployment "had no measurable effect on violent crime trends" and said the administration was claiming credit for declines already under way. CAP president Neera Tanden called the threat "a transparent attempt to silence us. We will neither cower nor bend in the face of it." General counsel Kevin Metz said the defamation allegation "is utterly absurd: truth is not and cannot be defamation."

What it means

The sum is the message. $5bn is not a damages estimate, it is a legal-fees estimate aimed at a think tank with a fraction of that in annual budget, and the demand letter does the work whether or not a complaint is ever filed. CAP's refusal to retract means the next move is either a filing or nothing, and a filing would put the crime statistics themselves in front of a court.

World Politics

Iran's "Economic D-Day" package is still unpublished, one day out

What happened

The Treasury's sanctions office published nothing on Saturday or Sunday. Its most recent action, dated Friday, is a pair of Venezuela general licences easing telecommunications supply and permitting contingent contracts for investment in the sector. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent's promised detail of the Iran secondary-sanctions measures — which he has described as "the greatest coordinated economic isolation in the history of the world" — is still expected Monday. Iranian officials escalated in the interval: Mohsen Rezai of the Supreme National Security Council said any country joining the restrictions "is considered an enemy", while President Pezeshkian defended the June memorandum with Washington.

What it means

A week after the threat, no oil purchaser and no Chinese entity has been designated, and the one thing Treasury did publish loosens sanctions on a different country. China takes over 80% of Iran's shipped oil, so a package that does not touch Chinese buyers is not a blockade regardless of the language around it. The Iranian statements are official claims and confirm intent, not capability.

Watch next

Monday, at ofac.treasury.gov/recent-actions. Whether any Chinese refiner is named is the only question that matters.

Seven killed in Ukraine on Saturday as Kyiv strikes Russian logistics

What happened

Russian attacks on Saturday killed at least seven people and injured 31 across Ukraine, including a strike on the border crossing with Moldova; Ukrainian air defence reported downing or suppressing 182 drones, six cruise missiles and three surface-to-air missiles. Overnight into Sunday, Ukrainian drones hit an Ozon logistics centre in Orenburg, an oil refinery, an airbase and the Yeysk oil terminal in Krasnodar Krai; a warehouse fire in Samara Oblast forced more than 500 employees out. Ukraine's General Staff claimed 1,310 Russian casualties in a day. All strike results and tallies are the claiming side's own figures.

What it means

The target set is the story. Ozon warehouses and fuel terminals are not military installations in the usual sense; they are the commercial logistics Russia's war economy runs on, and hitting them deep inside the country is a range statement as much as a damage one.

Watch next

Russian refinery throughput data, which is the only independent read on whether these strikes are working.
