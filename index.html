AI

Anthropic's IPO week opens with nothing new on the record

What happened

Books close Wednesday at 10 a.m. ET, trading starts Thursday, completion Friday, ticker “ANT”. Nothing new was filed over the weekend: the most recent Anthropic document we can evidence on EDGAR is the second amendment to the S‑1, and nothing carries a 14 or 15 November date. The one number circulating all weekend — that the order book is about six times oversubscribed — traces to a Bloomberg report of 12 or 13 November sourced to unnamed people, itself revised up from “multiple times” earlier in the week. No named source and no filing has confirmed an oversubscription level, and none has confirmed a price.

What it means

Oversubscription chatter three days before pricing is roadshow weather. It says demand is strong; it does not say where the price lands. The filed range is “estimated” and “may be varied”, and the underwriters have not agreed to underwrite the shares — so the first number that will mean anything arrives Wednesday morning.

Nvidia's Tuesday print is the first test of AI capex without a founder-chief

What happened

Nvidia reports its third quarter on Tuesday after the close, with the call at 2 p.m. PT — its first results since Jensen Huang resigned, and with Colette Kress presenting as both chief financial officer and interim chief executive. The guidance set in August was $108bn, plus or minus 2%, explicitly excluding China data‑centre compute. Street consensus figures circulating this weekend for revenue and adjusted earnings per share could not be stood up against their own compilers, and the three we saw disagree by basis as well as by a cent or two, so we are not printing them as settled numbers.

What it means

Two questions sit on top of each other. Whether the August guide held is the easy one. The harder one is whether a company with no permanent chief executive can set a January guide the market will trust — and that is the one that moves the stock.

Cantor Fitzgerald cut its target to $250 while keeping an Overweight rating, per StreetInsider. The widely repeated figure for HSBC's old target, $365, appears in one place and sits alongside a separate service carrying a different HSBC action at $185 from $195, so we are printing the new target and not the old one. Target cuts attributed to Stifel and Baird, and a count of “at least ten” analysts moving to Hold, could not be corroborated anywhere.

What it means

This is a downgrade on governance rather than on numbers, three trading days before the numbers. The Street is marking down the vacuum at the top, not the demand underneath — which is why a strong quarter may not fix it.

Business

The airlines are still paying for a four-hour stoppage

What happened

FlightAware counted more than 6,400 US flights cancelled and more than 8,000 delayed on Saturday, captured at 9:35 p.m. ET — a full day after Friday's ground stop was lifted. Delta, United and American each cancelled roughly a quarter of their Saturday schedules and JetBlue close to 45%. By Saturday night, 875 Sunday flights had already been scrubbed. For Friday itself, Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy gave 9,161 cancellations and 7,292 delays at the moment the stop lifted; FlightAware's own Friday count, via CBS News, was 9,159 and 7,190.

What it means

A four‑hour stoppage is producing a three‑day schedule, because aircraft and crews finish the day in the wrong cities and duty limits do the rest. No carrier has put a dollar figure on it, and we could not source published waiver terms from any airline — which is itself telling this far into the disruption.

A listed prime contractor gets named before any finding

What happened

Duffy said the failure began while L3Harris Technologies was replacing old copper wiring with fibre, leaving routers overloaded. At a Saturday press conference he said: “We're working to determine whether we had incompetence, meaning was it user error or installer error from L3Harris,” and “or did they install a faulty router? We haven't determined what the fault was.” He also said “We had over 1,000 connection attempts every four to five seconds,” which he said disrupted communication with aircraft and some radar. L3Harris said it was supporting the FAA's investigation and “takes the nationwide ground stop and its impact on travelers, airlines and air traffic operations seriously.”

What it means

A cabinet secretary naming a prime contractor before any technical finding is published is unusual, and it does two jobs at once: it locates blame outside the agency and it supports the funding ask. For L3Harris the near‑term exposure is reputational; the contractual question only opens once someone publishes a cause.

US

Three explanations for the outage, and still no published finding

What happened

Duffy's account is a contractor's fibre installation overloading routers. CBS News reports senior government sources saying the FAA's preliminary investigation believes the problem was “limited to Cisco 600 series routers that were manufactured between 2001 and 2006” — and, on the same page, multiple sources saying an update to the private communications network connecting the air traffic system caused a reboot, a crash and an outage. CBS adds that its sources are receiving conflicting information about the cause. PASS, the technical workers' union, puts it at two routers updated Friday, believed to be in Arkansas and Texas. One wire account has 17 of the FAA's 22 en route centres affected.

What it means

A fibre swap, a batch of 20‑year‑old routers and a network update are not three versions of one story; they are three different failures with three different owners. Until the FAA publishes something, every one of them is an official's characterisation or an anonymous source, and none is an engineering report.

A named official rules out a cyberattack, with the hedges still attached

What happened

Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin told reporters outside the White House on Friday that “there was no indication, as of yet, that this was a nefarious act against us” and that “It was just a telecommunication outage, as of right now.” At a later briefing that evening he said: “This is so far what we've seen. There is actually no cyberattack. We're still working through that process.” He has separately said it appeared the failure was “mechanically driven”. On the other suspicion, Duffy said of the FAA's new SMART tool: “There's no possible way SMART would have impacted what happened.”

What it means

Yesterday we wrote that no official had ruled a cyber attack in or out in their own words. That is now out of date: Mullin has, on the record and by name, which beats the unnamed law‑enforcement officials it rests beside. But “as of yet”, “as of right now” and “so far” are doing real work in those sentences, and the investigation is open.

The outage becomes a $50bn appropriations ask

What happened

Duffy called for $50bn for air traffic control, up from the $30bn the administration had been asking for, against the $12.5bn Congress approved last year. The department says it has awarded $4.3bn in contracts and spent $1bn of it. Representatives Larsen and Carson said in a joint statement they would “hold the administration accountable for what happened to make sure this chaos does not happen again.” Bryan Bedford, the FAA administrator, told reporters in Washington on Saturday that “No facilities went dark. Nothing was fully lost,” and that the worst was behind the system.

What it means

Friday converted a long‑running infrastructure complaint into a number with a constituency behind it. The politics are unusually easy — nobody runs against air traffic control — but $50bn is four times the existing authorisation, and a figure announced at a press conference is not a figure in a bill.

World Politics

Bahrain breaks its silence

What happened

Bahrain's Interior Ministry issued two alerts within about three hours early Sunday, the first at roughly 4:30 a.m. local time, warning of a potential threat and telling residents to head for the nearest safe location — Al Jazeera quotes the ministry only in fragments, never as a statement. The Bahrain Defence Force then said its air defences had thwarted several Iranian aerial attacks. The Revolutionary Guard, through Fars, said it had launched six drones at a US air‑defence radar at a base in Riffa. No casualties or damage were reported and the US military had not commented, per Al Jazeera. RFE/RL reported explosions at about 4 a.m. and a further series around 6:30 a.m.

What it means

Manama said nothing on Friday, when CENTCOM said it shot down two Iranian drones over Bahrain. It has now spoken, and what it describes is an attack on its own territory rather than on a US asset sitting there. That is a different political problem for a government that has spent the episode saying nothing at all.

A quieter night over Ukraine

What happened

Russia launched 132 drones from 18:00 on Saturday, by the Ukrainian Air Force's account — Shaheds, 78 of them jet‑powered, plus Gerbera drones and Parody‑type decoys — from Bryansk, Oryol, Kursk, Millerovo and Primorsko‑Akhtarsk, and from occupied Hvardiiske in Crimea and occupied Donetsk. As of 08:00 Sunday the Ukrainian Air Force said 106 had been shot down or jammed, 61 of them jet‑powered, with strikes recorded at 20 locations. The figures are preliminary and several drones were still in Ukrainian airspace. No missiles were reported and no casualties were given; a drone attack in Dnipro district set a private house on fire without injuries.

What it means

132 is at the low end of the past week, after nights of 123, 117 and 96. The number worth watching is the mix, not the total: jet‑powered Shaheds were 59% of the launch, faster and higher than mobile fire groups were built to catch.

ASEAN opens in Manila with the chip supply chain on the street

What happened

Ferdinand Marcos Jr opened the 49th ASEAN summit on Sunday, with nine heads of state attending. Donald Trump is due on Tuesday, near the close, with a Quad meeting expected on the sidelines; Australia's Anthony Albanese is to sign a defence pact with the Philippines. Nearly 20,000 police were deployed. Police arrested protesters demonstrating against Pax Silica, the US supply‑chain initiative aimed at countering China in semiconductors and rare earths, whose flagship Philippine project is a 1,620‑hectare AI manufacturing hub north of Manila. Arrest counts differ: nine in Manila and at least 17 in Muntinlupa, per witnesses cited by the Manila Times; five arrested and two hurt, per GMA, whose sourcing we could not establish.

What it means

This is what industrial policy looks like when it lands somewhere with residents. A chip and rare‑earths initiative is now a land question in Luzon and a protest issue at a summit Trump is flying into, while Manila signs defence deals with his allies in the same week.
