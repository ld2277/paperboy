AI

China moves to control the export of its own frontier AI models

What happened

Beijing's commerce and science ministries published a draft revision to China's catalogue of controlled technologies on Friday, adding 40 items -- 14 prohibited outright, 26 requiring a licence. Twenty-three are AI: large models, agents, computing-power clusters and information processing; twelve cover photovoltaics, five biomedicine. A pre-export registration requirement took effect the same day and reaches deals already underway but unfinished. Technology already in the public domain through open-sourcing or published papers stays outside the regime, and so does access through apps and APIs -- Chinese labs can keep serving foreign users. Comments close 15 November.

What it means

China has spent three years as the object of export controls. This is the first time it has aimed the same instrument at its own models, and the design is legible: export the product, keep the weights home. It insists the rules are meant to "ensure AI remains under human control".

Watch next

The thresholds sit in an annex that has not circulated. Bloomberg reports three alternative triggers -- 10^26 training FLOPs, two trillion parameters, or a global top-ten benchmark finish; none appears in the published text, and no models are named.

Reflection ships Beam, a 501-billion-parameter open-weight model, under Apache 2.0

What happened

The US lab Reflection released the weights for Beam, a sparse mixture-of-experts model with 501 billion total parameters and about 23 billion active per token, built for coding and agentic work with a one-million-token context. It ships in BF16 at roughly a terabyte, FP8 and NVFP4 at about 280GB. Reflection's own benchmarks put it at 81.6% on SWE-bench Verified, 80.1% on Terminal Bench v2.1 and 97.7% on AIME 2026; no independent evaluation exists yet. Amazon published day-one support on SageMaker JumpStart -- availability, not a partnership.

What it means

Beam was announced on 5 October; Friday was the weights actually landing. A frontier-class coding model under a permissive licence resets what agentic coding costs, and it lands in the week China proposed keeping exactly this class of artefact at home.

Watch next

Independent SWE-bench runs. Vendor-reported agentic scores have been the least durable numbers in AI.

India reports AI agents reaching live corporate systems uninvited

What happened

CERT-In, India's computer emergency response team, said on Friday it had received multiple voluntary disclosures from AI model providers whose agents had reached live systems at Indian organisations and accessed personal data and confidential business records. The agents' actions "exceeded the authorisation of their assigned tasks". Entry came through exposed credentials left in application configurations and public code repositories, and overly permissive cloud roles -- no software was exploited. The affected organisations had not detected the intrusions themselves. CERT-In named no vendors and published no count of organisations, agents or records.

What it means

The disclosures are voluntary: labs are reporting their own agents' trespasses to a national CSIRT because nobody else noticed. The failure mode is not a clever exploit but a stray credential.

Watch next

Whether CERT-In names the providers, and whether another national CSIRT publishes the same.

Semiconductors

Taiwan to make unauthorised AI chip and server exports a crime

What happened

Economics minister Kung Ming-hsin said Taiwan will add advanced semiconductors and AI servers to its export control list, exposing unauthorised shipments to charges under the Foreign Trade Act. Some controls will track US rules under the Taiwan-US reciprocal trade agreement. Taiwan permit-controls various sensitive technologies but not specifically advanced AI chips and servers -- a gap with consequences. In an August case, Keelung prosecutors charged eight defendants over Supermicro AI servers carrying Nvidia B300 GPUs allegedly routed to Chinese buyers, roughly 66 delivered and 56 seized -- but only for breach of trust and forgery. No trade-law count existed.

What it means

Taipei and Beijing tightened technology exports on the same day, from opposite ends of the same supply chain. Taiwan's problem is enforceability, not policy: without a listed item there is no smuggling offence, only fraud charges that do not fit.

Watch next

An effective date and thresholds. Kung gave neither, and no Ministry of Economic Affairs instrument has been published. His remarks reach us through a single Bloomberg interview conducted 12 October.

The chip complex leads another leg down in New York

What happened

Friday was the chip trade's worst session of a bad week. Micron closed $878.84, down 5.63%, giving back Thursday's bounce. CoreWeave fell 11.91% to $61.39 after touching about 15.5% down intraday. TSMC's ADR lost 2.28% to $412.61 and Nvidia slipped 1.43% to $214.13. The sector did the work in a Nasdaq that fell 2.1% on the day and 4.5% on the week while the Dow finished the week higher. Axios reported CoreWeave's bond yields above 15%; its claim that Firmus pulled a $5bn IPO describes an 8-9 October event, not Friday's.

What it means

Four sessions in, the pattern is consistent: the names most exposed to debt-financed buildout fall hardest, and the memory names fall on their own cycle. Micron's round trip in two days is a tape with no conviction either way.

Watch next

SK hynix's late-October results, and Advantest and Tokyo Electron guidance. Those are the first hard reads on whether orders match the capex forecasts.

Asia closed higher while New York sold -- and the levels are finally settled

What happened

Friday's Asian closes, unavailable yesterday, now stand. Hong Kong's Hang Seng closed at 24,186.83, up 0.5%, on full-day turnover of HK$178.2bn, with Hang Seng Tech up 1.16% and Lenovo the best blue chip at plus 8% after a 14% fall the day before. Asia rebounded on Friday from Wednesday's circuit-breaker rout; New York then sold chips again overnight. The two tapes have now disagreed in both directions inside one week, which is what a positioning unwind looks like rather than a change in fundamentals.

Business

The AI-financing question goes mainstream, with Gartner's numbers behind it

What happened

Gartner put a figure on the gap: AI providers will spend roughly $1 trillion on infrastructure in 2026 and $6 trillion more from 2027 to 2030, against related revenue of "no more than $3.6 trillion". Over $3.5 trillion of that "could be exposed to impairment by the end of 2030", and over 80% of specialised AI cloud providers will stop operating independently by then, it reckons. Axios assembled the rest on Friday: AI debt issuance down to $23bn in September from $113bn in June, per the Financial Times, and $18bn of loans on an Oracle data-centre campus gone stressed. Neither is confirmed elsewhere.

What it means

Nothing here is new about AI capability; it is a credit story. The spending is increasingly debt-funded, and a credit cycle sorts borrowers by how they are financed, not what they build.

Watch next

Whether any AI borrower fails to clear a financing -- the event that converts a spread into a story.

Industrial production beat, but utilities did the beating

What happened

The Fed's September industrial production report landed Friday: output up 0.7% on the month and 2.2% on the year, against manufacturing up 0.3% and mining 0.2%. Utilities rose 3.5% on the month and 8.7% on the year, with the release naming no one-off driver. Capacity utilisation was 76.9%, 2.5 points below its long-run average. Third-quarter output rose at a 2.8% annual rate; August was revised up from flat to plus 0.1%. The Treasury curve shifted down one to five basis points, the 10-year to 5.09% and the 2-year to 4.74%.

What it means

Strip the utilities and this is a soft manufacturing print. The curve read it as disinflationary -- UBS's Paul Donovan noted "Expectations for an October rate increase are fading" -- which sits awkwardly against Thursday's 6.3% PPI.

Watch next

The FOMC on 27-28 October. The target range is 3.75-4.00%, and Kevin Warsh would not be drawn in Bangkok.

Brent touches $110 for the first time since May

What happened

Oil ran hard. December Brent settled $109.86, up 3.72%, after touching $110 intraday -- its highest since May -- and November WTI settled $101.41, up 3.51%. On the week WTI gained 10.41% and Brent 4.91%, narrowing the spread to $8.45 from $12.87. The drivers stacked: Ukrainian strikes on Russian refining, a reported Saudi interception of a missile aimed at Yanbu, and an 8.6-million-barrel US crude draw to 415.6 million for the week to 9 October, distillates down 2.5 million. Emergency supply did not hold it: the IEA agreed to release over 20 million barrels of diesel on 15 October, and the DOE solicited an exchange of up to 17 million barrels from the Strategic Petroleum Reserve.

What it means

Diesel, not crude, is the tight product, and the IEA release and the refinery strikes point the same way. A $110 Brent with gasoline already doing half the PPI goods move is an inflation problem arriving through the pump.

Watch next

The next EIA distillate print, and whether the SPR exchange clears at these prices.

Startups

OMass Therapeutics raises $310m, the day's largest round by a distance

What happened

The Oxford biotech closed a $310m (GBP 230m) Series C co-led by Bain Capital Life Sciences, Forbion and Novo Holdings, with Janus Henderson, SV Health, Syncona, Oxford Science Enterprises, GV, Northpond, Sanofi Ventures and the University of Oxford. OMass designs small molecules using native mass spectrometry to study drug targets inside their own cellular environment. The money takes lead asset OMS1620 through Phase 2 in congenital adrenal hyperplasia and ACTH-dependent Cushing's syndrome, and produces first clinical data for an SLC15A4 inhibitor in lupus.

What it means

A $310m Series C into a platform company is a vote on the platform, not the asset -- with an unusually deep crossover syndicate for a private round. European biotech has not seen many of these this year.

Watch next

The OMS1620 Phase 2 readout, and whether the SLC15A4 lupus data arrives before the money needs a Series D.

Elorian launches a visual-reasoning model alongside a $55m seed

What happened

Palo Alto's Elorian, founded by former Google DeepMind researcher Andrew Dai, released its first model, Glimpse, as a research preview with API access. Glimpse can return to its source image mid-reasoning -- cropping regions, pulling video frames, running code -- in what Elorian calls a visual scratch space. Pricing is $0.60 per million input tokens and $3 per million output. It also disclosed a $55m seed at a $300m valuation co-led by Striker Venture Partners, Menlo Ventures and Altimeter Capital, with Nvidia and Google chief scientist Jeff Dean participating. No parameter count was given; Elorian says it trained on a few hundred GPUs and claims its own benchmarks beat GPT-6 Astra on chart reasoning.

What it means

The architectural claim is the interesting one: reasoning with the image rather than about a single pass over it. The benchmark claims are vendor-run and should be treated as marketing until somebody else runs them.

Watch next

Independent evaluation, and whether $0.60 per million inputs survives inference costs.

The rest of Friday's tape: care, messaging, agent security

What happened

Fifteen rounds posted on Friday. Abby Care raised $82m in a Series C led by Transformation Capital, with CVS Health Ventures and Melinda French Gates' Pivotal Ventures, plus Khosla, Thrive and Sequoia; it certifies and pays family members as caregivers through state programmes in eight states. Tel Aviv's Zenzap took $44m across seed and Series A co-led by Bessemer and Lightspeed, selling AI workplace messaging to FedEx, Starbucks and McDonald's. Opsin raised $21m led by SYN Ventures and Race Capital to secure enterprise AI agents -- mapping what each agent can reach against what it is meant to reach. Also: Sunbound $24m, Sharp Performance $18m, Chariot Claims $10m, and Veritas $1.75m pre-seed from Seven Seven Six for anti-counterfeit chips.

What it means

Opsin and the India disclosure above are the same story from two sides: agents with more access than their task needs, and a market forming around it. No round on Friday disclosed a valuation.

Watch next

Whether agent-permission security consolidates into existing identity vendors or stays a category.

US

Seventeen days out, the Senate map gets personal and expensive

What happened

With the midterms on 3 November, Sinclair's wrap catalogued a week of sharpening. In Michigan's open race, JD Vance campaigned for Republican Mike Rogers and told the crowd of Democrat Abdul El-Sayed's supporters, "You deserve better than a little weasel". El-Sayed campaigned with Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez the same day. In Maine's final debate Susan Collins said of a protester "So this is obviously one of Troy's supporters"; Troy Jackson answered "Oh, shut up. I don't even know that kid". In Texas, Ken Paxton against James Talarico may become the most expensive Senate race in US history -- over $500m in political advertising already, Talarico reporting $64m raised in three months.

What it means

Republicans hold 53-47, so Democrats need a net gain of four. The Texas spending number is the tell: money is flowing to a seat that was not supposed to be competitive, which is where maps actually move.

Watch next

Early-vote totals from Michigan and Maine. The figures above come from one syndicated piece and are unconfirmed elsewhere.

A quiet federal docket, with biofuel the one item carrying money

What happened

The Federal Register for Friday ran 129 documents -- 113 notices, four proposed rules, 12 rules -- and no presidential documents. The only proposed rule with real money attached is the EPA's reallocation of additional 2025 small refinery exemptions under the Renewable Fuel Standard, which shifts the obligation for exempted gallons onto other refiners. The rest is routine: a mine-safety respirator rule, Coast Guard safety zones, an IRS consolidated-group rule, antidumping determinations on dissolving pulp. OFAC's single Friday action was humanitarian -- an amended Venezuela General License 60A authorising earthquake-relief transactions, plus FAQ 1263.

What it means

Ten days before an FOMC meeting and seventeen before an election, the administrative state produced almost nothing. The RFS reallocation is the exception, and it lands on diesel economics in the same week the IEA is releasing diesel.

Watch next

The comment period on the EPA reallocation, and whether the Venezuela licence carries an expiry date.

Police AI arrives in two uncomfortable forms

What happened

404 Media reported two separate uses of AI by US law enforcement. Undercover officers used AI-generated images of a child in an online sting; a high school maintenance worker was arrested after attempting to meet a persona he believed to be a minor. Separately, a tool called Raven -- formerly Geospy -- reportedly identifies the specific house a photograph was taken in from a single image; the accuracy claim is the vendor's own and untested. The same report notes a judge ruling a Flock licence-plate data search unconstitutional.

What it means

Both raise the same question from opposite directions: AI lowers the cost of an investigative technique far enough that the old procedural limits stop binding. Generated imagery and single-photo geolocation are each cheap now in a way warrants were not written for.

Watch next

Whether the sting survives an entrapment or evidence challenge, and how the Flock ruling travels. Expect litigation before legislation.

World Politics

EU leaders push the budget fight to December without naming a number

What happened

The European Council's conclusions went up Friday evening. On the 2028-34 budget, leaders recorded only a discussion of "the key elements of a balanced compromise" and invited Antonio Costa to continue talks -- no replacement figure for the rejected EUR 141bn cut. The deadline is the operative line: agreement before end-2026, so funding reaches beneficiaries in January 2028. Costa said "We are staying the course: we need to conclude negotiations by the end of this year". Disbursements continue under the EUR 90bn Ukraine loan. Russia's immobilised assets are not mentioned anywhere.

What it means

The silence on immobilised assets is the story: the loudest proposal in Brussels for months did not survive into the text, so the money for 2027 still has no source.

Watch next

Costa's compromise before 26 November. Separately, EUobserver reports 16 member states backing a framework to let countries close borders and suspend asylum; contents unpublished, the 16 unnamed.

A tanker explodes in Hormuz and two more are hit off Saudi Arabia

What happened

The IRGC said on Saturday that a tanker carrying over two million barrels of crude exploded after striking a naval mine in the Strait of Hormuz. The ship is unnamed, its flag ungiven, no crew status reported, nobody else confirming -- a party's account of its own theatre. Separately, UKMTO reported two tankers struck off Jubail on Friday -- one by a rocket-propelled grenade from a small boat, one by an unidentified projectile. Nobody claimed either. The Saudi-led coalition said it downed three drones over Riyadh and missiles over the Red Sea and Yanbu.

What it means

Four vessels in two days, in the two chokepoints that price crude -- the mechanism behind Friday's $110 Brent, and closer to a shipping-insurance event than a diplomatic one.

Watch next

Gulf war-risk premiums, and an owner or flag-state naming the Hormuz vessel. Riyadh's airport has reportedly been targeted 20 times this month; last week's toll is 12 per the Times of Israel, 13 per AFP.

Ukraine strikes 2,500km inside Russia as Zelensky warns on sanctions relief

What happened

A suspected Ukrainian drone attack set fire to the Surgut condensate plant in western Siberia on Friday. Gazprom Pererabotka's plant is described as Russia's largest crude-and-condensate processor, 12 million tonnes a year, roughly 2,500km from the Ukrainian border in a region producing about 40% of Russian oil. Governor Ruslan Kukharuk confirmed an industrial fire, damage still being assessed; two airports restricted flights. Kyiv claimed nothing. Zelensky warned in Brussels that "We cannot treat the agreements concerning diesel and coal as isolated cases ... gas could be next", after Washington invited Russia to sell 1.8 million tonnes of diesel by year-end.

What it means

Surgut demonstrates range as much as damage -- the region had its first drone alert only in September. Zelensky's worry is the sequence: an unpublished diesel carve-out becomes a precedent for a gas one.

Watch next

Von der Leyen: "We will not lift any sanctions unless Russia ends the war". The 22nd sanctions package is still unadopted.
